# Why each deck is built the way it is

Each launch deck's reasoning, settled with Martin: what the deck is, the job each card does in it, and the ideas rejected for it, with the reasons. The cards themselves (rarity, strength, text) live in `animal_kingdom/data/cards.json` and show in the deck docs (`../cards/decks/`), generated from it; a reason behind one card's number sits in that card's note in the card table, beside the number. This doc names cards and never restates them, so it can't drift from them.

## Food OTK

The hoard (Martin, 2026-10-08): a fortress around a bomb. Magic's Approach of the Second Sun, Hearthstone's Freeze Mage and Quest decks. Yuki stakes everything on one animal for two turns; the rest of the deck reaches a stake worth multiplying and builds the walls that make those two turns survivable. The fun is the siege: a visible timer, the opponent throwing everything at the walls, and the puzzle of building an answer for each way their deck can reach the squirrel. It beats midrange and slow interaction (answers one at a time), loses to speed (Food Aggro, Fish, Den Rush), and gives the meta a reason to carry hard answers. Food from Roars can't be touched, so it loses to Food Aggro by design.

Yuki triples the stake rather than doubling it: doubling is a net gain of only the stake, two turns late, so fair decks just raced it in the imagined games and neither side touched the other. Tripling makes the siege compulsory. Removing Yuki loses the whole stake, and covering it pauses its timer; that risk and the two turns are the opponent's window. The legendary Octopus's Scout always finds it.

The walls answer each way to reach the squirrel: a removal, a ground cover, a cover from behind the lines. Each must be beaten by no existing card and leave the opponent an ordinary answer (a cover with more strength, or a removal ability): an animal that can never be covered is an unbreakable wall. "When this is removed" on a small body never fires (opponents cover small animals instead), and a 0 blocks nothing. The Porcupine and the Hedgehog kill coverers, the Armadillo armours its neighbours, the Toad and the Jellyfish poison, the Golden Orb-Weaver catches flyers and roamers landing from behind the lines, and the Stag Beetle is a plain Armor wall. The Meerkat is the sentry: every enemy placed beside it is a card, so the siege refills the hand. Ash carries every keyword: Spikes takes the first coverer at once, which cancels that cover's Poison, and every later coverer is poisoned. One crossroad the opponent never truly takes, not a wall: the region breaks through any other corner. Iris copies a wall where the fortress needs one; elsewhere it copies any deck's best piece, an enemy's too. The copy takes strength, keywords and text but not the Roar, so copying Yuki does nothing.

Every 10 food on the board by turn 4 is about 30 at the finish, and food arriving on turn 7 counts for nothing: the stake has to be early. The Tortoise is the early stake (food for each Armor animal, with the Armadillo's neighbours and Ash), the Black Bear the slow draw that fits a deck waiting to fire.

Rejected: a common paying food for each Armor, Spikes or Poison animal (clunky; the Tortoise counts Armor alone), and its "each of your animals next to this" rewording (generic food that Food Aggro wants more); the Acorns (late, random food; banked for a deck that draws all game, Egg Control first; Jay as the animal, since Squirrel is taken).

## Food Aggro

Burn (Martin, 2026-10-08): Magic's Red Deck Wins, Hearthstone's Face Hunter. Food is the win counter itself, so a deck whose cards each gain about 10 food is a clock, not a combo: there is nothing to assemble. Food from a Roar can't be undone, so every card is a chunk of the opponent's life and nothing on the board blocks it. The fun is counting to lethal: the "10 or more this turn" payoffs make sequencing matter, and the Beaver's extra action with Tally makes the burst turn. It preys on control and stall (removal touches nothing here); it loses to den pressure, hand disruption, covering its Rodents and the food thieves once they exist. It must stay fragile, or it's solitaire.

Imagined games (reasoning, not evidence) showed the clock runs on cards, not food: the hand is empty by turn 4. A card that draws one card is tempo, not value (a Draw action draws two); only cards that draw two or more, or make food over several turns, refill the clock. Running out of cards is this deck's best moment, not its end (Magic's Hellbent). A Roar checks the hand after its card has left it, so "if your hand is empty" means "if this was your last card".

The chunks are the Squirrel, the Chipmunk (cheek pouches: food now and next turn) and the Flying Squirrel; the Gopher, the Muskrat (the den defence) and the Groundhog pay off a 10-food turn. The Hamster is the board answer, the one body that covers an enemy and stays big: the hoarder grows with the hoard, in hand too. The Woodpecker makes the turn's next Roar happen twice, a second chunk or a second Tally. The Beaver's action comes back next turn, so it is a free body. The Dormouse is the refill the empty hand needs, a card at every Dusk after a food turn (it comes out at dusk to forage); Dusk comes before region income, so only the turn's own food wakes it. The legendary Macaque refills the hand to the opponent's (Hearthstone's Divine Favor: a big refill against control, a small one against aggro); Tally is the finisher; Big Gus, played last into your own board, repeats each adjacent Rodent's Roar, capped by the grid at four, and every Rodent the opponent covers is one Roar fewer. A repeated Tally multiplies the turn, so Tally is Big Gus's dial.

Rejected: the Octopus carrying the extra action (a sea animal in the Rodent deck); Greywhisker and the Naked Mole-Rat (Den Rush's); the Mouse and other draw-one cards (tempo, not value); a "protect it and it wins" food engine (a control card); "discard your hand, gain food for each"; "gain food for each card your opponent has more than you"; a Squirrel that shuffles Acorns into your deck (20 food eventually, in a deck that barely draws); the Mouse raid ("gain 10 food for each enemy next to this": the Mouse is already a card, and a common animal); a Dormouse paying 10 food every Dusk (the protect-it-and-it-wins engine again); doubling the turn's food on Tally (a common's effect on a legendary), and doubling it every Dusk (too strong).

## Circle of Life (the Cockroach deck)

Symmetric removal that punishes the opponent more than you (Martin, 2026-10-22): the Sperm Whale and its kin remove everything around them, yours included, and you don't mind, because your animals come back (the Cockroach, the Roach King) and every removal pays (the Vulture, the Python). The legendary, The First of Many, is the big reset that amplifies it: the board's top layer cleared, and what survives is yours, the cockroaches crawling back and your buried animals resurfacing. What makes it worth playing: the opponent's interaction feeds it (their covers are your deaths), each turn is a puzzle (how many deaths and payoffs out of two actions), and it wins from behind, on food the opponent can't block, while they seem to hold the board.

How it's built:

- Every piece of fodder pays on its own death; the payoffs multiply that.
- Food scales with the turn's deaths, since food is the win clock; cards don't (the Vulture checks once a turn), since per-death draws would turn the loops into five cards from one action.
- Payoffs count deaths as they happen, not the Remove Pile, since the best fodder leaves the pile (the Cockroach goes back where it was, Ember into the deck).
- Each loop costs a placement, so two actions a turn bound it; any "play another animal" effect needs an audit against these loops.

The fodder: the Cockroach and the Roach King come back where they were, the Earthworm leaves two Worms that each draw when they die, Ember returns to the deck. The Dart Frog is the bomb: Reach hops it into the opponent's animals, Poison kills whoever covers it, so only a removal clears it, and any removal (the Mantis, the Piranha, Pestis, the Sperm Whale, the Locusts) sets it off, taking every animal beside it; two side by side chain. The deaths: the City Spider gives one every Dawn with no action spent (Dawn, not Dusk, so the turn's Piranha and Vulture see it; Hearthstone's Dreadsteed beside the Cockroach), the Praying Mantis turns one into cards, and Pestis takes a whole stack. The payoffs: the Piranha's frenzy after a death (a tiny animal that kills anything, so no big animal can carry it), the Python growing with every removal, the Vulture landing once the turn has seen enough deaths, the Raccoon bringing an animal back from the Remove Pile. The Sperm Whale lands at the front, since its own neighbours die too.

The First of Many (Martin, 2026-10-22): "Roar: cover the board with Locusts." The Locust is a token, 0: "**Wild.** In 2 turns, remove this and the animal under it." Wild is no one's: it connects no one and holds no region (the plague eats the harvest: neither side's regions pay while it sits there), any animal covers it, and its timer ticks every turn, so it eats at the start of the caster's next turn, after the opponent's whole turn to see it, and the Hyena, the Vulture and the Piranha count it. A covered Locust waits, so covering one spares that crossroad; Armor sits through it; the legendary goes with its swarm. Rejected: "remove all other animals" (a Hearthstone board wipe out of nowhere, everything balanced around it), Doomsayer's "Dawn: remove all animals" (one cover stops it or nothing can, and a wipe seen coming only makes both sides skip a turn), wipes on each owner's own Dusk (the caster's board goes first and the opponent walks into the den), Locusts owned by the animal's owner (a board of 0s the opponent covers first) or by the caster (every region theirs at Dusk), a swarm that spreads crossroad by crossroad (far too slow), a death-split Locust (below the Toad, a common), and anything that removes only adjacent animals (placement rarely puts a card next to more than one or two enemies, and the board is full by turn 5).

Pool cards built for it: the Hyena turns the turn's deaths into food; the Tarantula is a death every turn and a growing body, steered by what you place next to it (strength has no ceiling, so past 10 only a removal ability answers it, as with the Python); the Sea Turtle is free fodder every turn with no card or action spent, which is action economy and needs the den audit (a turtle one crossroad from the opponent's den front sets up a capture); the legendary Sloth doubles every Dusk effect, bounded since each fires once a turn (watch it if every Dusk deck runs it); the legendary Cuckoo, the chick that shoves the host's eggs out of the nest (no Flight: a nestling can't fly, and with Flight it would be removal anywhere), is small removal on an enemy and a free placement on your own spent animal; it's no den burst, since removing your own animal from the chain cuts it off.

Expected weak points, untested: few cards that kill your own (Spiders die to any cover), and few bodies bigger than a 4.

## Cats

Midrange tempo on the front foot (Martin, 2026-10-23): Hearthstone's Hunter, never reacting to the opponent. Cats win the fight for the board by covering, and each cover is worth more than the opponent's placement because the body comes with a second half: the Jaguar and the Serval remove, the Lynx and the Bobcat draw, Alley Cat and Prince Leo with Princess Lea bring another body. King Theron makes every cover a removal and Queen Adira turns removals into cards. Removal is a rider, not the deck's signature.

The Cat pool is meant to build along a spectrum, as Hunter ran from Face through Hybrid to Midrange with Dr. Boom and Ragnaros: an aggro build on the cheap Cats and their extra bodies, a midrange build with top end. Top end here means an engine, an effect that keeps paying every turn it stays on the board, never a big body: a body does nothing after it lands but hold its crossroad. An engine in the backline is safe and pays for it with conditions (Theron, Adira); one that works only next to enemies is exposed by its own design. The legendary Leopard is the engine that hunts, roaming and eating each turn. The legendary Tiger is the man-eater at the front: it lands as a predator and takes a random neighbour at every Dusk until the opponent covers it with an 8 or removes it. It stands at 7 so the ordinary 8s can answer it.

The fast build empties its hand by turn 4, and the Ocelot is its refuel: three cards from an empty hand. A Cat carries it though a tagless animal would suit a draw engine better; the ocelot has no trait of its own, so it is the effect's skin. Rejected for the fast build: anything that pays for standing next to the opponent's den (a Dusk draw there, Flee there): a connected animal in front of the den wins with the next placement, so the opponent answers it at once and nothing ever stands there for a turn.

Rejected for the Tiger: "this turn, your Cats can be placed on enemies of any strength" (a ceiling only small bodies need: the Fish legendary that covers by Fish count does it); "Dusk: gain 5 food for each enemy buried under your Cats" (too strong, a food engine safe in the backline, and against half the deck, whose removal leaves nothing buried); a big body that leaves two cubs when covered (Savannah Highmane's surface: leftover bodies matter where bodies attack, here they only fill crossroads); "Dusk: remove the strongest adjacent enemy" (Martin chose the random one).

## Canines

The pack that roams the board, buffs itself on the board (Primates buff in hand) and hunts by position. High skill: each turn is a puzzle of moves and placements across two actions. Built on Roam ([`../rules/keywords.md`](../rules/keywords.md)), a game-wide keyword that Canines carry most. Count-based effects (stronger the more of them) belong to the Fish.

The Wolf is the plain roamer, the Orca and the Dhole the big ones (Dhole packs take prey bigger than themselves), and the Fox draws as it hunts. The Bush Dog, the Coyote, the African Wild Dog and the legendary Wolf grow the pack on the board; the Street Dog and the Badger feed the roamers (the badger digs the prey out for the runners, as badgers do for coyotes). The Jackal removes by position, an enemy two Canines surround. The White Wolf gives a free roam each turn, since the deck is short of actions, not cards; the Black Wolf feeds the pack, bounded by one roam per animal per turn. The legendary Leopard drops onto its prey from a tree, then waits: landing by Reach leaves it unconnected, so it roams again once the chain reaches it.

Open: "your animals next to this have Roam" waits for a legendary in a deck without roamers of its own.

## Fish Token Aggro

The swarm (Martin, 2026-10-08). Gwent's Arachas, Hearthstone's Murlocs: one card becomes a school, and every Fish makes the others better. The opponent should feel outnumbered, not outmuscled: each fish is small, but covering them one by one costs a card and an action each. A 2×2 square of Fish is a region, so the packed school wins on food. It preys on slow decks and on decks that answer one threat at a time; area removal (Rhinoceros, Hippopotamus) is its predator, which puts Giants on top of it.

How a game goes (agreed with Martin, 2026-10-08): the deck's resource is the empty board, which is full by turn 4 or 5. Turns 1–3 flood it and close regions (two regions, 25 food a turn, reach 100 around turn 6 or 7). Turns 4–6 hold them: the count cards are big by then, and removal clears the coverers off your corners; late Fish land on your own Fish (always legal), firing their Roars and leaving a backup beneath. If the opponent wins the board war by turn 6, Fish loses, on purpose (Hearthstone's Token Paladin): the cards that fill empty crossroads are dead topdecks late, the price of the best opening in the game. Its meta role: it punishes greed in the opening (Handlock, Food OTK, Giants). Food Aggro races off the board, Hoofed holds one region slowly with big animals; Fish takes many regions, fast and fragile. One buff card at most, or it's the old Canines again.

Payoffs count every Fish you control: a plain count keeps the swarm feeling, and a smooth count (each Fish a bit more) plays differently from Colony's thresholds. Adjacency cards stay wherever they make sense; on map_b a crossroad has at most 4 neighbours, so "for each adjacent Fish" pays about 1, at best 2. Legendaries are drawn at a random point, often after the board is full, so each must work then; the opening is the commons' job.

The Sardine and the Sunfish fill the empty board (the sunfish lays more eggs than any other animal), and the Remora brings another Fish beside a Fish. The Tuna and the Cod count the school; the Mahi-mahi is its one buff. The Barracuda and the Swordfish clear coverers, and their removals switch on the Great White Shark's Reach. The Manta Ray turns covers into cards. The legendary Jellyfish poisons the Fish beside it (young fish shelter among the tentacles; "your other Fish have Poison" would make every corner uncoverable, the herd's rejected card again); the legendary Tuna is the closer and the late reload (the fish people know as food); the legendary Manta Ray draws while the school holds two regions; the legendary Piranha lets the small Fish win crossroads back once the board is full (the school strips something far bigger; Snow Leopard's shape).

Fish's predator is weak on this map (area removal reaches only the edge of a school built at home); Giants need a way to hit the middle of a school. Open Ocean's roster gains Sardine, Tuna, Cod, Remora, Barracuda and Mahi-mahi, to settle in the roster pass after the decks. Rejected: Baby Fish spawners at legendary and rare (empty crossroads exist only in the first turns), a food common (Food Aggro's ground), Goldfish (a pet), Salmon (a river fish, for River and Lake).

## Hoofed Graze

The herd on its pasture (Martin, 2026-10-08): the nearest card-game match is Magic's Pillowfort, a deck that makes attacking it unprofitable while a slow engine wins, here on a piece of land. Hold regions with grazers, win on food; grazing is all or nothing, so one covered corner breaks the region and switches off every grazing bonus, and the opponent always has a weak corner to aim at. It beats decks that win by covering (Cats, Canines, Fish) and Giants' coverers; it loses to decks that just race. The plan that's fast enough is both home regions (six crossroads, shared corners at the den front), not one.

The deck is two halves. Fleers fill the empty pasture early and keep their value in their Roar, so running home is a refund and the Roar comes again: the Zebra's herd food (zebras grazing among the wildebeest; it rewards a tight pasture and speeds the food race), the Gazelle's card and food (a far smaller animal than a deer, so a small body and a stronger effect), the Deer's Fawn (Bambi, the herd's speed: two corner-holders for one action; covered, the doe bolts home and the Fawn stays, and the replay places another). Standers hold the corners and are placed on top of the fleers (Flee triggers only on an enemy cover): the Wildebeest's grazing food, the Giraffe's wall on the opponent's turn, and the Okapi's card flow (the one-of-a-kind forest giraffe with zebra legs; the Impala was a Gazelle lookalike). The Cape Buffalo raises the corners on the opponent's turn. The Warthog drives the predator off a covered animal, and the animal underneath is safe; the Boar answers a big animal on bare ground; the Honey Badger the biggest.

The legendary Wildebeest is the Great Migration, its regions richer; the legendary Boar its mirror, a tech card against every deck that farms regions (wild boars tearing up fields is what farmers know them for); the legendary Giraffe, the tallest animal, sees the lions first, good in any reactive deck; the legendary Zebra counts kinds, not numbers, a synergy no other family has.

Rejected: a regroup rare ("play a Hoofed animal from your hand": spends the hand this deck is short of); the fleeing payoff, the white stag that re-lands on an empty crossroad (there rarely is one) and the herd that bolts back to hand (it wastes your own actions, the opposite of Flee); "enemy regions next to your grazing animals produce no food" (an animal next to a region is one of its corners, so no enemy region is ever next to yours); Flee on animals whose value is where they stand (a fled corner can't be retaken, since the coverer is bigger); Stealth, Armor and Spikes while grazing (Food OTK's identity); a den that can't be captured while grazing; a +1 anthem legendary; a herd bull that stops covers on your other grazing animals (on the back corner nothing reaches it: a lock); a stampede and a 3-of-4-corners region (clunky); the Zebra kicking an enemy back to hand (Zebra is its stripes), and its flee that sent the coverer home too (both players spent an action undoing each other); the Gazelle with Reach and Flee and no Roar (Flee saves nothing worth saving), pronking that costs the coverer 10 food, a fawn of its own, a draw when it flees (the Deer's ground); the Deer as the rutting stag that removes while grazing, with antlers that grow while grazing (the Giraffe is already a 9 on the opponent's turn), or as a Hoofed tutor.

## Giants

Feed the giants (Martin, 2026-10-08): Magic's Eldrazi Ramp, Hearthstone's Ramp and Big Druid. The ramp resource is food, which is also the win counter: every point a giant eats is a point not counting toward 100, so Giants win on the board and the den, rarely on food. Hungry animals eat each turn or shrink; the food cards keep them fed. The fun is landing bodies nothing can cover and running the feeding economy. It beats boards of small and mid-sized animals by covering them with bodies they can't cover back (Fish, Cats, Canines: Giants prey on Fish by covering their region corners, not by area removal, which reaches only a school's edge); it loses to races it can't touch (Food Aggro), unconditional removal and fortresses (Spikes kills any coverer).

Imagined games (reasoning, not evidence): the board dominance works from turn 4, once the chain delivers the bodies; the feeding economy bites only when the ramp pieces come late (three giants eat about 11 a turn); the Oxpecker is a big ramp piece (about fifteen animals of 8 or more in the starting deck).

The bodies: the Elephant, the vanilla giant (the bigger, the hungrier), the Rhinoceros and the Hippopotamus that clear small animals around them, the Moose that roams, the Whale Shark that draws; the Blue Whale is a whole turn for a 10 that never eats. The feeders: the Oxpecker, the Dung Beetle, the Anteater (it rips open termite mounds: the bigger the mound, the bigger the meal) and the Sloth. The legendary Rhinoceros clears everything around it; the legendary Oxpecker, placed between giants, is guarded by them (oxpeckers live on giants, eating their ticks), and an enemy has to reach one of its free sides; Azamandulai is the tortoise of the fable, capping each player's food per turn so the slow deck catches up. The legendary Sperm Whale is Moby Dick's white whale (the name evokes it without citing it): on map_b its Roar is close to a full wipe, everything touching your herd. A 10 can't be covered, so its power has to be in the arrival and nothing may keep working after it: "an enemy placed next to this is removed" locked whole regions for decks without a removal Roar. Dials if too strong: "of strength 7 or less", "next to your Hungry animals", or only its own neighbours.

Rejected: the Anteater as an anti-Colony card (Martin: a card that only punishes one deck is bad design), and "Giant" in its name (it's a 4).

## Handlock

Train in secret, then unveil (Martin, 2026-10-08): Hearthstone's Handlock and hand-buff decks (Don Han'Cho, the Grimy Goons); Magic has almost nothing like it. Two hand themes, hand size and training cards in hand, paid off by thresholds, triggers and removal. The fun is hidden power: the opponent sees how many cards you hold, never how strong they are. Strength past about 8 buys almost nothing on the board, so training has to pay off through thresholds, triggers and removal, not raw size. It beats midrange and strength-gated removal; it loses to speed and to hand disruption (Egg Control's Black Swan and Magpie).

Hand size: the Macaque removes from a full hand, the Peacock grows with it, the Hummingbird and the Stork fill it (the stork brings the younglings, one per family). Training: the Baboon, the legendary Baboon and the legendary Gorilla raise the hand, the Orangutan duplicates a trained Primate, the Tarsier fetches one and trains it. The payoffs: the Chimpanzee's removal (the chimp with its stone or stick: the tool is as heavy as the arm that swings it), the Gorilla's draw past a threshold, the Caterpillar turning a buff into a card and a Butterfly.

The legendary Butterfly ripens by itself, in hand or on the board, and buffs only speed it up (Martin, 2026-10-22): evolving on buffs alone made it win-more, its fourth buff arriving when the hand was nearly empty, every buff taken from a real threat. Its stages stack, each adding one thing (Egg, Caterpillar, Chrysalis, Butterfly, Monarch), so its power climbs evenly: below a common, then common to rare, rare to legendary, above an average legendary. Stealth, not Armor: a chrysalis is famous for hiding, and butterflies hide too. The legendary Eagle comes with its mate: every buff to either counts for both, the deck's outlet for training; trained, each eats an enemy anywhere. Sharing one number avoids the loop "when one gains, the other gains" would make. An Apex Predator can't land on an empty crossroad or a den, so Flight never captures.

## Egg Control

The Mosquito, with Flight, shrinks any enemy on the board, setting up the flying Hawk (a 5 becomes a 3) or the Black Mamba (a 7 becomes a 5); the Viper does the same job next to your chain only, a midrange card. Grimm takes from the opponent's Remove Pile: the deck removes more than any other, so that pile holds their best cards, and Grimm is a Bird, so the Bird Egg's Scout finds it.

Rejected: a Magpie that filters (a weaker Raven), the Monitor Lizard (anti-token removal that replaces itself: good everywhere, nothing special here; eating your own Egg only throws away its hatch).

## Colony outside the precon

The precon's cards all carry a Colony-shaped condition (4 or more, a Queen, a Worker, two of the same), so none of them leaves the deck and only the Hornet enters it (Martin, 2026-10-21: a Colony player is forced into all four legendaries, and the tag has one card outside the precon). Wanted: about two legendaries, a rare and three commons that carry the tag and work in other decks. The shape that travels: the card's own condition is one other decks meet for their own reasons (a food threshold, Flight, its own copies), and the tag is only a tag, a body Colony counts toward its thresholds. "If you control another Colony animal" is the precon in disguise and is not used. New Colony cards are castes of the species already in the deck (Bee, Bumblebee, Wasp, Hornet, Ant, Army Ant, Termite): the deck splits its species by caste, never by subspecies, so a Leafcutter Ant can't stand beside Worker Ant and Soldier Ant.

Queen Melissia says "whenever you place" (Martin, 2026-10-21), not "play": Vesper arriving from the deck and a Vulture landing pay her too; moving (Roam) still doesn't.

In the reserve, effects agreed (Martin: "sounds much better effect-wise"), animals and numbers to settle: the Guard Bee (a third of the Hedgehog's body, and it flies), the Forager Ant (the Worker Ant's food on the Muskrat's condition), the Queen Wasp (Queen Melissia keyed to Flight at a rare's rate, so the legendary stays ahead), the Scout Bee legendary, the dancer (Tally turns food into food; this turns it into cards), and the Ant legendary, the farmer (the Dung Beetle's Dusk for any wide deck).

The Naked Mole-Rat (Savanna roster) is the one eusocial mammal and would carry Colony beside Rodent, the first card two families both want.

## Open topics

- The bloodsuckers drain strength: the Mosquito's −2, the Leech's swap, the Tick's effect still to design, likely a slow drain.
- Taking the opponent's food belongs to thieves (Seagull later, the monkeys) and gets its own session.

## Banked ideas

- A tagless rare closer (2026-10-23): "Roar: if you control more regions than your opponent, gain 15 food." The fast decks' food finisher; waits for a tagless animal whose trait is holding territory.
- Leech (Jungle, 1): "Roar: swap strength with an adjacent animal." It drains its host: the host shrinks, the leech swells. At 1 it can't cover anything, so it lands only on an empty crossroad or one of your own.
- The decoy (Lapwing, needs a common or rare Lapwing): "Enemy abilities that choose an animal must choose this, if able." It fakes a broken wing to lead predators from the nest. A protector of any deck's engine.
- The lure (a legendary Firefly): "Roar: your opponent's next animal must be placed next to this, if able." The femme-fatale firefly copies another species' flash and eats the males that come. First candidate for the Arachnid traps deck.
- The herd over its young (Martin likes it, for another deck): "Your animals placed on top of your own animals have +2 strength." A mechanic only this game has; Fish landing on their own Fish late is the first fit.
- Groundhog Day (a later Groundhog legendary, in a slower deck; the species has its Food Aggro common): "Roar: gain 5 food. Dawn: this Roars again." The day that keeps repeating, an everyday idiom. Not on the common: Food Aggro wants this turn's burst, not an engine.
- Clownfish (Coral Reef, later): its own trait, safe in a stinging anemone. Electric Eel (Jungle) is the stun.
- Platypus (Australia, later): its family tag is "All", counting as every family (Martin, 2026-10-08).
- Hermit Crab (Coast, later): Armor plus something about moving into another's shell.
- The Opossum, a mother gathering her young onto her back, is the City's seed for the Marsupials' "carrying the young" identity when Australia comes.
- The Cougar and its effect wait for Mountains.
