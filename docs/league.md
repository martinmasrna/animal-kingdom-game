# The league: a simulated ladder populated by bots

Martin, 2026-10-24: "what we need to do is simulate a real ladder environment and populate it with bots while we have too little human players for some real testing. And if this means self-evolving decks, custom evaluation functions for each deck, or rather self-evolving neural networks, and so on, then let's do it." And: a feedback loop where tens of thousands of self-play games are judged by twenty games of one matchup against him is no feedback loop.

## What it replaces

The ladder itself exists: `web/ladder.py` rates people and every bot-and-deck pairing with Glicko-2, the ranked queue matches a person with the bot pairing nearest their rating (`server.join_ranked`, `Ladder.nearest_bot`), and `sim/ladder_seed.py` seeds the bots' ratings from a round robin of the forty-five pairings, anchored so a reseed keeps the scale people were rated on. Of Martin's games against the Expert, 1,085 were ranked games on that ladder, against decks the matchmaking chose. What was missing on 2026-10-24: the seed dated from 20 October and the Expert had changed four times since, so the bots sat on the ladder at the strength of an Expert that no longer existed, and every person's rating was fitted against those stale numbers; the bot pool was fifteen hand-picked decks that never changed; and a bot change was judged by a paired benchmark of those fifteen decks against three fixed opponents, twenty games a deck, with the self-play rounds drawing their games from the same fifteen decks. The league is the seeding made continuous and alive: one population that grows, every game rated on the ladder's own scale, and a reseed shipped whenever the population or the pilot changes.

## The design (settled with Martin, 2026-10-24)

The rule above every other: the ladder acts exactly as a real ladder of people would. A mechanism belongs in the league only if a human ladder has its equivalent.

**A player is one pilot and one exact 30-card list.** It has one rating, its own games and its own leaderboard row; its list is never edited and the player is never removed. On a human ladder the rating follows the person across decks because the skill is theirs; for bots the skill is the pilot, the same for every Expert pairing, so a pairing's rating can only mean the list's worth under that pilot, and a changed list is a different player. A list identical to an existing one is that player. Every new list enters under all three pilots, so the lower bands get the new decks too.

**One rating store.** The web ladder (`web/ladder.py`, Glicko-2) is the only table. A bot-versus-bot game played by the runner is reported to the server and rated like a person's game; nothing is ever reseeded. A new player enters at 1500 with the wide uncertainty a person starts with, places itself through bot games, and is not offered to people until it has placed. The two seeds of 2026-10-24 were the bootstrap, the last ones.

**New lists come from three sources, and from nothing else.** Martin's own, whenever he hands them over, no limit and no special treatment. Tinkering: a one-card child of an Expert player, chosen from that player's own games, the card whose drawn-against-not-drawn win rate over the last two hundred games sits outside the noise ("I keep losing when I draw this"), replaced by what wins in lists like it around its rating or against the opponents it has been meeting. Netdecking: a near-copy of a top-band list with one to three cards changed, what a player lower down does when the same deck keeps beating them. There is no exploiter caste: each list adapts to its own neighbourhood, and neighbourhoods differ by band, which is what keeps the pool from collapsing onto one deck. Nobody is retired for being weak: a weak list under the Easy pilot is a Beginner's opponent. A child must differ from every existing list, and matchmaking never hands a person the same lineage twice running, as it never hands them the same deck twice.

**The pace is the only dial** (proposed, to be read again after two weeks of ladder data): tinkering is triggered by the statistic, about one list in eight a week at first; netdecking is scheduled, one list in twenty of the middle bands a week; fifteen to twenty bot-born lists a week, the pool doubling in a month. Compute is not the constraint: a thousand players need five thousand games a day, two hours of the Mac.

**What the ladder gives back.** Which lists are competitive at near-human level and under which pilot, read off the table Martin is rated on; the meta by band (archetype shares, what the top meets); and per-deck card statistics (drawn against not drawn) as card signals for Martin, never as something the bots fix.

## What the literature says it costs and where it breaks

Fontaine et al. (GECCO 2019) evolved Hearthstone decks with MAP-Elites: 10,000 evaluations of 200 games each per class and strategy, on a 500-CPU cluster, pilot a turn-local search like our TurnBot. Their stated main critique of their own method: the decks are evolved for that pilot's playstyle. Saravanan and Guzdial (2024) rebuilt the Pokémon Showdown metagame with a team-builder, a battle agent and an environment, 450,000 battles per scenario; with metagame knowledge the discovered meta matched the real one closely, from a blank slate it overlapped 35%, and they note the meta depends on the agents' skill level. Hernandez et al. (2020) measured metagame balance from a win-rate matrix of MCTS agents at 50 games a matchup and reported 96 hours per experiment. Balduzzi et al. (2018) and Omidshafiei et al. (2019) show Elo misranks cyclic populations and give Nash averaging and alpha-rank as the fixes.

Our engine plays about 3,000 untimed Expert games an hour on the Mac with seven workers, so a league generation of 60 players and 20 games each takes about 25 minutes, and a week of nights is a few hundred generations. A 48-core rented box is ten times that for about one euro an hour; the decision to rent is Martin's.

Where it breaks, and the answer in the design: pilot bias (decks tuned to how the bot plays): human games feed the evaluator and exploiters hunt the leaders. Elo cycles: the win-rate matrix and Nash averaging sit beside the Elo. A meta that depends on skill: the league's rating bands hold weak and strong pilots at once, as a real ladder does. Noise: ratings persist across generations, so twenty games a generation is enough because nothing is decided on twenty games.

## The build, in order

1. Done 2026-10-24: the reseed for the live Expert, everyone's placement games, the leaderboard one row per pairing, the pool widened to 43 decks and 129 pairings with its bootstrap seed.
2. The server endpoint that accepts a bot game result and a new list (authenticated, bots only), rating with the same Glicko update; provisional players kept out of people's matchmaking until placed.
3. The runner: plays by matchmaking on the Rust engine, games where uncertainty is largest first, and reports every result. The Mac's own Bradley-Terry fit stays only as a check.
4. Tinkering, then netdecking, at the pace above. Martin's lists enter through the same endpoint.
5. Pilot candidates enter as players on every pool list and ship when their pairings outrate the live pilot's beyond the noise; the paired benchmark stays as a cheap first screen.
