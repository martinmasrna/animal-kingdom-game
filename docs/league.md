# The league: a simulated ladder populated by bots

Martin, 2026-10-24: "what we need to do is simulate a real ladder environment and populate it with bots while we have too little human players for some real testing. And if this means self-evolving decks, custom evaluation functions for each deck, or rather self-evolving neural networks, and so on, then let's do it." And: a feedback loop where tens of thousands of self-play games are judged by twenty games of one matchup against him is no feedback loop.

## What it replaces

The ladder itself exists: `web/ladder.py` rates people and every bot-and-deck pairing with Glicko-2, the ranked queue matches a person with the bot pairing nearest their rating (`server.join_ranked`, `Ladder.nearest_bot`), and `sim/ladder_seed.py` seeds the bots' ratings from a round robin of the forty-five pairings, anchored so a reseed keeps the scale people were rated on. Of Martin's games against the Expert, 1,085 were ranked games on that ladder, against decks the matchmaking chose. What was missing on 2026-10-24: the seed dated from 20 October and the Expert had changed four times since, so the bots sat on the ladder at the strength of an Expert that no longer existed, and every person's rating was fitted against those stale numbers; the bot pool was fifteen hand-picked decks that never changed; and a bot change was judged by a paired benchmark of those fifteen decks against three fixed opponents, twenty games a deck, with the self-play rounds drawing their games from the same fifteen decks. The league is the seeding made continuous and alive: one population that grows, every game rated on the ladder's own scale, and a reseed shipped whenever the population or the pilot changes.

## The design

A league is a population of players. A bot player is a pairing: a deck and a pilot (search plus evaluation). A person is a profile. Matchmaking pairs players of similar rating, as a real ladder does, so the strongest play the strongest and a new entrant finds its level quickly. Every game updates one Bradley-Terry fit (`tools/ladder/ladder.py`), and the league also keeps the pairing-against-pairing win-rate matrix, because a card game has cycles (aggro beats control beats combo) and a single Elo hides them; Nash averaging over that matrix (Balduzzi et al. 2018) separates "strong across the field" from "strong against one deck".

Decks evolve. A deck is a genome over the legal card pool; a mutation swaps one to three cards (geometric, as Fontaine et al. 2019). A child enters the league at a provisional rating and is culled after its first forty games if it sits below the median. Fitness is the league rating itself, never a fixed opponent set: Miernik and Kowalski (2022) found in-population evaluation beats a fixed strong opponent in tournament play and that a fixed weak opponent is worthless, and Fontaine et al. found decks evolved against weak adversaries win less in direct comparison than decks evolved against elites. Diversity is kept MAP-Elites style: niches by deck descriptors (average cost, region mix, creature share), with the best deck per niche protected from culling, so the league does not collapse onto one archetype. Exploiters (AlphaStar's league, Vinyals et al. 2019) are decks evolved with one fitness: win rate against the current top three. They are the metagame's counter-decks and they keep the leaders honest.

Pilots evolve. The shared slot network retrains on league games, which are diverse by construction (every deck the league holds, at every rating level), so the pool drift that narrowed the self-play rounds cannot recur. A candidate pilot enters the league as a new player on every league deck and is rated like everyone else; it ships when its rating across the league's decks beats the live pilot's beyond the fit's uncertainty. Per-deck evaluation heads come after that, as candidates in the same league. Cards never change for the bots (docs/bots.md, invariants).

People play in the same league already: a ranked web game is the person against the bot pairing the matchmaking picks near their rating, and the result moves both ratings. The league's job on that side is to keep the bots' rows true: a new seed (`web/ladder_seed.json`, versioned, applied once by `Ladder.apply_seed`) after every pilot change and every league epoch, anchored on the pilots that did not change so people's ratings keep their meaning. The goal, the best pairing at about 2100 on the ladder Martin is rated on, is then read off one table.

## What the literature says it costs and where it breaks

Fontaine et al. (GECCO 2019) evolved Hearthstone decks with MAP-Elites: 10,000 evaluations of 200 games each per class and strategy, on a 500-CPU cluster, pilot a turn-local search like our TurnBot. Their stated main critique of their own method: the decks are evolved for that pilot's playstyle. Saravanan and Guzdial (2024) rebuilt the Pokémon Showdown metagame with a team-builder, a battle agent and an environment, 450,000 battles per scenario; with metagame knowledge the discovered meta matched the real one closely, from a blank slate it overlapped 35%, and they note the meta depends on the agents' skill level. Hernandez et al. (2020) measured metagame balance from a win-rate matrix of MCTS agents at 50 games a matchup and reported 96 hours per experiment. Balduzzi et al. (2018) and Omidshafiei et al. (2019) show Elo misranks cyclic populations and give Nash averaging and alpha-rank as the fixes.

Our engine plays about 3,000 untimed Expert games an hour on the Mac with seven workers, so a league generation of 60 players and 20 games each takes about 25 minutes, and a week of nights is a few hundred generations. A 48-core rented box is ten times that for about one euro an hour; the decision to rent is Martin's.

Where it breaks, and the answer in the design: pilot bias (decks tuned to how the bot plays): human games feed the evaluator and exploiters hunt the leaders. Elo cycles: the win-rate matrix and Nash averaging sit beside the Elo. A meta that depends on skill: the league's rating bands hold weak and strong pilots at once, as a real ladder does. Noise: ratings persist across generations, so twenty games a generation is enough because nothing is decided on twenty games.

## The build, in order

1. Reseed now (2026-10-24): the round robin of `sim/ladder_seed.py` with the live Expert, anchored on Easy and Normal, shipped as the new `web/ladder_seed.json`. Then the league runner grows out of that tool: a persistent population (players, every game appended, ratings refitted), matchmaking by rating instead of a full round robin, games on the Rust engine, the bot pool plus Martin's decks from his logs as the first population.
2. Deck evolution: mutation, provisional entry, culling, niches, exploiters.
3. Pilot candidates as league entrants, and the retrain loop on league games. The paired benchmark stays only as a cheap first screen for a pilot change.
4. Rated web games on league decks, when Martin says how they should feel.
