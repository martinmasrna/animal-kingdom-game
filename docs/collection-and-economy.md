# Collection and economy

How players get cards, and how the game makes money. All of it is open: nothing here has been decided. What follows is Martin's reactions, quoted with their date, and the agent's reading and recommendation. Every card is unlocked for everyone today.

## Where the discussion stands (2026-11-11)

Martin's principles, as his reactions (2026-11-11):

- **No gacha.** He wants to avoid random packs that border on casino mechanics: "I don't want any like gacha mechanics with opening packs and like a lot of randomness". If there are packs at all, they follow strict rules (for example 1 legendary, 1 rare and 3 commons, only cards you don't own yet).
- **Money can buy a card directly.** "if someone wants to skip the like manual grind and like spend a lot of money to save time and effort, they should absolutely be able to do so. But it shouldn't be required". Example: "I want to play Ragnaros. I want to be able to buy Ragnaros directly".
- **Free players grind, and the grind is pleasant.** The grind is mostly passive: playing, quests, achievements. There's no forced tedious PvE ("Hearthstone Mercenaries ... was horrendous").
- **One shared resource.** Tutorials, daily quests, achievements and season rewards all grant one currency, and that currency crafts any card directly.
- **Short paths to rewards.** Achievements pay out directly, not through Hearthstone's chain of gems, track, pack, disenchant and dust. They might pay in cosmetics only.
- **Animated premium cards,** after Gwent beta's, unlocked by an achievement tied to that card. Martin: "the only game where I actually cared about cosmetics of the cards".
- **A PvE challenge per legendary** that tells its story and rewards the legendary or its premium version. He flags it as expensive to build.
- **Pacing:** not a full collection in the first week, not six months. Profit isn't the goal ("We're doing this just to, you know, create a good game"), but there needs to be a plan for how the game makes money.

The agent's recommendation (2026-11-11), not yet judged by Martin:

- **Keep every card unlocked until there are enough players for a collection to pay off,** probably until the first expansion.
- **One currency with direct crafting; no packs at all.**
- **PvE gives cosmetics and currency, never cards needed to compete.**
- **One entry per card in the collection,** with the premium version as a switch on it.
- **Card animations are earned only, never sold;** other cosmetics are sold.
- **Legendary challenges double as the puzzles** in the "few tactical moments" item in [`later.md`](later.md).
- **Test animating one legendary soon,** because it decides whether the cosmetics plan is real.

Open choices:
- whether anything is locked before the first expansion;
- whether premium animations are earned only or also sold;
- what the legendary challenges reward;
- the numbers (how fast a deck, and a full set, can be earned).

The legendaries' stories wait on the world session: the world, its societies and who the individuals are stay open (see `AGENTS.md`).

## The research round (agent, 2026-11-11)

Martin asked how other card games handle the collection, especially the ones that break the Hearthstone and Magic model of random packs, disenchanting, crafting and quests for gold. The agent answered from its own knowledge, without new web research; details from the last year or two may be stale. Its answer, verbatim:

> I answered from what I already know, without new web research, so details from the last year or two may be stale. I've marked the claims I'm least sure of. In short, the market isn't one model: there are about six, and the most interesting ones are the departures.
>
> ## 1. The standard: random packs plus crafting
> Used by Hearthstone, Gwent, Shadowverse, Yu-Gi-Oh Master Duel and Magic Arena.
>
> - You earn gold, buy random packs and turn duplicates into dust to craft the cards you actually want.
> - The randomness is the money engine. Paying is what speeds up the hunt for a specific card.
> - **Magic Arena's variant:** packs drop "wildcards", one per rarity, and each one turns into any card of that rarity. There's no general dust.
> - **Hearthstone has softened over the years:**
>   - a free core set for everyone;
>   - no duplicate legendaries;
>   - catch-up packs that only contain cards you don't own;
>   - free starter decks.
> - **Yu-Gi-Oh Master Duel** sells "secret packs" aimed at one archetype, so the randomness is narrowed to the deck you're after.
>
> ## 2. Legends of Runeterra: the generous model
> - **No packs for sale.** Cards came from a weekly vault you filled by playing, from a reward track per region (you picked the faction you wanted to grow) and from wildcards.
> - **You could also buy any card directly.** Money went to cosmetics.
> - **Reception:** praised as the fairest card game economy, then cut back. Riot said in 2023 that the game didn't earn enough, laid off much of the team and moved the focus to the single-player mode (Path of Champions). It's the clearest evidence of what a generous model does to revenue.
>
> ## 3. Marvel Snap: progress through cosmetics, no rarity that affects play
> This one is the real outlier, and closest to the "non-traditional" you're asking about.
>
> - **Every card is equal in play.** Rarity exists only on the cosmetic side.
> - **Progress comes from upgrades.** You spend boosters and credits to upgrade a card's look: borders, 3D effects, animation. Each upgrade raises your "collection level", and climbing it hands out new cards. Spending on looks is literally how you progress.
> - **Cards arrive in pools.** Pool 1 is simple, and later pools get rarer and more complex, so the collection doubles as onboarding. ("How Marvel Snap teaches" is already on your study plan.)
> - **No crafting, no dust, no trading.** Later additions:
>   - weekly Spotlight Caches that put featured cards on offer;
>   - tokens to buy specific cards from a rotating shop.
> - **Paid side:** a monthly season pass whose new card you get early, plus cosmetic bundles.
> - **Criticism:** completing the late pools is slow and grindy. Uncertain: the exact cache and token details have changed several times.
>
> ## 4. Fixed products, no randomness
> - **Living Card Games** (physical Netrunner, Arkham Horror LCG): every box has the same contents. You buy an expansion and own all of it.
> - **Artifact** (Valve, 2018) is the cautionary tale: you paid for the game, then bought packs too, and traded on the Steam market. Players rejected it. The relaunch made every card free, and it died anyway.
> - Uncertain: I believe a few smaller digital games went "buy the game, own every card", but I can't name one that was clearly successful.
>
> ## 5. Opening packs as the product
> - **Pokémon TCG Pocket (2024):**
>   - free packs every day;
>   - "Wonder Pick": take a card from another player's opened pack;
>   - trading added later.
> - The game itself is thin. Opening and collecting are the experience, and it earned a fortune in its first months. It shows that collecting can be its own fun, separate from competing.
>
> ## 6. Avoiding the collection
> - **Hearthstone Battlegrounds and drafting modes:** everyone plays from the same pool, so owning cards doesn't matter.
> - **KeyForge:** every deck is unique, computer-generated and can't be changed. The collection *is* the deck.
> - **Clash Royale** is the warning in the other direction: cards also *level up* in power, so in competitive play spending money buys strength.
>
> ## The choices underneath all of these
> - **How cards arrive:** randomly, randomly within a target, in a fixed order (pools), or all at once.
> - **Whether you can aim at a specific card,** and at what price (dust, wildcards, tokens, direct purchase).
> - **What money buys:** speed toward the cards, the cards themselves, or only looks. Snap and Runeterra show that looks alone can work only if the cosmetics are good enough.
> - **Whether the collection also teaches** (Snap's pools) or is just a store.
> - **Whether collecting is its own pleasure** (Pokémon Pocket) or only a gate to playing.

## The agent's response (2026-11-11, verbatim)

> You're close to Legends of Runeterra, plus one thing it never had: animated premium cards as achievement rewards. I agree with most of it. I'd add one point you didn't raise, and it changes the order of everything else.
>
> ## The point you didn't raise: don't lock the collection yet
>
> Unlocking cards is a reason to keep coming back that runs out. Once you own everything, it's gone until the next expansion. A gate like that only pays off when there are enough players for a slow climb to be worth it.
>
> Right now it would cost you exactly what you need most:
> - **Deckbuilding is your design signal.** The cards played in games are your design data, and that data comes from people building decks. Lock half the pool and you get fewer kinds of deck in the games you learn from.
> - **Few players means few matches.** Making newcomers grind before they can build what they want shrinks a small player base further.
> - **Newcomers would face finished decks with a starter.** Your game already gives a weaker player less luck to win with, so that matchup would feel even harsher.
>
> As I understand it, everyone owns every card today. I'd keep it that way until there are players to spare, probably until the first expansion. Design the economy now so it's ready, but turn it on later.
>
> ## Your economy: agreed, with three adjustments
>
> **One currency, earned by playing, spent on any card directly, and money can buy it.**
> - This is simpler than Hearthstone's chain of gold, packs and dust, and honest.
> - Paying to skip the grind is legitimate when it buys exactly the card you want.
> - Your fixed packs with no duplicates are fine. With direct crafting they're unnecessary, though, so I'd leave packs out entirely rather than build a gentler casino.
>
> **1. The money ceiling.** Without randomness, the most anyone can spend is the price of a complete collection. That's honest, and fine for you, but it means the other income has to come from cosmetics and expansions. That's a reason to protect the cosmetics plan below.
>
> **2. PvE may give looks and currency, but never cards you need to compete.** That one rule is what prevents another Mercenaries. Tutorials can grant the starter decks, since that's onboarding, not a grind.
>
> **3. One entry per card in the collection.** The premium version is a switch on the card, not a second copy. That fixes your Snap clutter complaint.
>
> ## Animated premiums as achievement rewards: the best idea here
>
> An achievement gives an animated version of its card, shown right away, with no chain of currencies before the reward. It brings you back, gives you something to show off, and nudges you toward cards you'd otherwise skip.
>
> One choice to make knowingly: **earned only, or also sold?** A badge is only worth showing off if nobody could buy it. So I'd make card animations earned only, and sell other cosmetics: board looks, card backs, profile pictures. Your league plan already makes the profile picture the place where achievements show.
>
> **Feasibility:** turning one still image into a short looping video is a solved, cheap task now. The risk is your bar. The painting must not change, only the air, water and light move around it. That's exactly how your card art already works. One legendary is the test, and it takes about an hour.
>
> ## Legendary challenges
>
> These overlap with the backlog item on tactical moments from earlier. A legendary's challenge is a hand-built position you solve with that card, which is a puzzle. One piece of work then serves the puzzle problem, teaches the card and tells its story.
>
> - **Reward:** the animated version, not the card itself, per adjustment 2 above.
> - **The story part:** it waits for the world session, since the game's societies and who these individuals are remain open.
> - **Already in progress:** there are existing cloud sessions on puzzle mode and the legendaries' legends to build on.
>
> My recommendation: keep everything unlocked for now. Test animating one legendary soon, because it decides whether the cosmetics plan is real. Park the currency for the first expansion.
