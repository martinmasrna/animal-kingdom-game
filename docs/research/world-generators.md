# World generators: what existing worlds did

This file answers one question: for a set of well-known creature worlds, what single cause (an event or a law) did the world follow from, how does it show in the creatures, how are new creatures made from it, and was the world written before the creatures or after.
It reports what existing worlds did and proposes nothing.
Written 2026-11-22.
Where a claim rests on a secondary source, or on a search-result summary I could not open at the original page, the text says so; the list of unsourced claims is at the end.

## Dark Souls and Elden Ring (FromSoftware)

**Single cause.**
For Elden Ring the work states one: Miyazaki called the Elden Ring "the name given to a mysterious concept that defines the world itself" and said "this 'Elden Ring' has been shattered" ([Xbox Wire, 2019](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2019/06/09/hidetaka-miyazaki-and-george-rr-martin-present-elden-ring/)).
The Famitsu summary adds that fragments of it were taken by Queen Marika's children, which started a war ([Siliconera, secondhand from Famitsu](https://www.siliconera.com/elden-ring-miyazaki-interview-reveals-its-story/)).
For Dark Souls I could not find a one-sentence cause in the creator's words.
What Miyazaki lists in the Design Works interview are themes: "the world, life and death", "the meaning of fire", and three area images (gods and knights, chaos and demons, death and decay) ([Design Works interview, translated](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Dark+Souls+1+-+Design+Works+Interview)).
The in-game premise of a fading first flame is the game's own opening; I did not fetch a source for it.

**What follows visually.**
The Design Works interview ties creatures to those themes: "All demons are born from the fire of chaos" and "The demons are all based around the idea of Chaos"; Gravelord Nito carries "the image of death and decay"; the Gaping Dragon is built on greed but, Miyazaki says, was kept from being "either fat or have a huge mouth", and is backstoried as "part of an ancient race of mineral based life forms" ([same interview](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Dark+Souls+1+-+Design+Works+Interview)).
Named examples there: Gaping Dragon, Egg Carrier, Gravelord Nito, the Chaos Eaters (their inspiration is credited by a different interviewee, Satake, to Lovecraft's Great Race of Yith, not by Miyazaki).
Materials, colours and anatomy are not described in these interviews; the reading of Souls creatures as rot, ash and fused bodies is common but I did not find it stated by the creators.

**How creatures are generated.**
Miyazaki's own account is a brief, not a rule: he gives artists "just the most basic, essential information" and "a few simple 'image words'", then lets them draw, and he asks for a design that goes "against their preconceptions" (same interview).
The rule that a creature must follow a theme (chaos makes demons) is stated for the demons; that this generalises to every creature is my inference.

**Characters first or world first.**
Neither, by his account.
Asked about Demon's Souls, Miyazaki says he does not make games from the world: he starts from the game's systems and concept, then picks a setting that fits and adjusts it; dark fantasy is the setting that fits gameplay most easily for him ([Gigazine interview, 2012, in Japanese](https://www.gigazine.net/news/20120228-darksouls-miyazaki-interview/); the translation here is mine).
For Dark Souls he says "The map design was really what dictated everything else" and that rough maps were drawn once the function of each area was known, with creature concepts emerging after ([Design Works interview](https://darksouls.wiki.fextralife.com/Dark+Souls+1+-+Design+Works+Interview)).
For Elden Ring the mythos was written first, by George R.R. Martin, and the world "was constructed using this mythos and stimulus as a base" ([Xbox Wire](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2019/06/09/hidetaka-miyazaki-and-george-rr-martin-present-elden-ring/)).
Miyazaki has said Martin might be surprised how far the world moved from that start; this comes from a Game Informer interview that I only saw summarised by [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/uk/george-rr-martin-may-be-shocked-by-how-his-elden-ring-lore-has-been-twisted) and [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/action-rpg/game-of-thrones-author-george-r-r-martins-starting-point-for-elden-ring-evolved-so-drastically-that-hidetaka-miyazaki-reckons-hed-be-surprised-how-the-open-world-rpg-turned-out), not at the original.
Style of withholding: he says he is "not that good at" direct storytelling and prefers clues in items (Wired 2016, seen only via [FandomWire](https://fandomwire.com/im-just-not-good-at-hidetaka-miyazaki-admitted-a-storytelling-flaw-that-stops-thousands-from-enjoying-elden-ring-dark-souls/)); and that Dark Souls "has been completed by players" (Edge, via [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/miyazaki-says-dark-souls-has-been-completed-players-so-no-more-secrets-then)).
That is a technique for delivering a world, not a generator of creatures.

## Lovecraft's mythos

**Single cause.**
Lovecraft's own statement, to Farnsworth Wright on 5 July 1927: his tales rest on the premise that "common human laws and interests and emotions have no validity or significance in the vast cosmos-at-large" ([hplovecraft.com quotes page](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/quotes.aspx)).
The related craft claim in the same letter: to get "real externality" one "must forget that such things as organic life, good and evil, love and hate" exist (same page).
Underneath it, the essay opening: "The oldest and strongest emotion of mankind is fear, and the oldest and strongest kind of fear is fear of the unknown", and the weird tale needs "a malign and particular suspension or defeat of those fixed laws of Nature" ([Supernatural Horror in Literature](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/essays/shil.aspx)).

**What follows visually.**
Creatures are described as violating the human body plan rather than following a theme.
Cthulhu, in the story's statue description, is of "vaguely anthropoid outline" with an octopus-like head whose face is a mass of feelers, scaly rubbery body, claws and rudimentary wings, and the narrator says "The Thing cannot be described" ([The Call of Cthulhu text, Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/h-p-lovecraft/short-fiction/text/the-call-of-cthulhu); the quoted phrases came to me through a search summary of the text, so check the wording there).
The Wright letter also criticises fiction in which "the human form" and human passions are "depicted as native to other worlds" ([quotes page](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/quotes.aspx)).
"Atmosphere, not action, is the great desideratum" and "Prime emphasis should be given to subtle suggestion" ([Notes on Writing Weird Fiction](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/essays/nwwf.aspx)).

**How creatures are generated.**
No designer's rule exists; he wrote prose.
Inference from the letter and essays: the repeatable test is "would this thing still make sense if human values and the human body were irrelevant to it", which is a negation of the human template, not a positive template.

**Characters first or world first.**
The shared "mythos" is a label applied afterwards: Lovecraft never used "Cthulhu Mythos"; August Derleth coined it after his death, and Lovecraft sometimes called the linked stories an "Arkham cycle" ([hplovecraft.com on the Mythos](https://hplovecraft.com/popcult/mythos.aspx)).
The 1927 premise statement was written on resubmitting "The Call of Cthulhu" ([Yog wiki, secondary](https://yogwiki.cthulhueternal.com/wiki/Lovecraftian_Philosophy), seen via search summary), so the premise was stated alongside the first major creature story; I found nothing on which was conceived first.
In 1931 he called "Yog-Sothoth" "a basically immature conception, & unfitted for really serious literature" (letter to Frank Belknap Long, 22 February 1931, on the Mythos page above).

## Pokémon

**Single cause.**
There is no world-level cause.
There is a creator-level one: Satoshi Tajiri collected insects as a child ("Mr. Bug"), and in 1990, seeing two Game Boys joined by a link cable, he pictured creatures passing between them and being collected and fought ([Kotaku, "The Origins of Pokémon"](https://kotaku.com/the-origins-of-pokemon-5806664)).

**What follows visually.**
Not from one cause.
Sugimori's Gen 1 team worked within hardware limits: small pixel sprites meant designs had to be "easy to read" and simple ([Sugimori and Masuda interview, Lava Cut](https://lavacutcontent.com/sugimori-masuda-developer-interview/)); an analyst counts recurring habits such as roughly a third of Gen 1 with "angry anime eyes", three-clawed hands and triangular ears even on Blastoise and Venusaur ([Compton, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/evolution-of-pokemon-designs-gen-1); one analyst's reading, not a creator statement).
Named examples: Rhydon, Rattata, Zubat (Rattata and Zubat as common RPG-enemy archetypes per Compton).

**How creatures are generated.**
The creators describe no rule.
Sugimori in a 2000 interview: about 20 to 30 initial designs, "pretty lost" at first, then learning to give creatures more expressive faces; he later wanted one design "that wasn't so obvious" and "a more surreal design", which produced Unown ([Well Played translation of the 2000 interview](https://www.well-played.com.au/pokemon-artist-ken-sugimori-talks-design-in-newly-translated-2000-interview/)).
Sugimori: "Pokemon's made up of ideas from every staff member" (Lava Cut above).
Types: Sugimori says they came "in the middle of development" because battles "would be monotonous if Pokemon were simply 'strong' or 'weak'"; some Pokémon were then built around types and earlier designs were given elements (Lava Cut above).
Compton reads Gen 1 types as assigned mainly by how well they fit the finished design, which left the distribution uneven (Game Developer above; his reading).

**Characters first or world first.**
Creatures first, and the organising structure (types, the Pokédex completion goal) came mid-development; about a third of the creatures made so far were cut to fit data limits, and the starters were changed "about ten times" (Lava Cut above).
First designs: Sugimori names Rhydon, Clefairy and Lapras among the earliest; the interviewer's notes say Rhydon is the likeliest first design and call "the first three" a mistranslation (Lava Cut above).
Dr Lava's notes are the only support I found for Rhydon being first; the Smogon claim is unsourced ([Smogon](https://www.smogon.com/articles/pokemon-art-through-ages)).

## Hollow Knight (Team Cherry)

**Single cause.**
A setting premise, not a law: a once-thriving kingdom fallen into decay, with "the grandeur of the old world" seeping "through the rot", set beneath the surface ([Game Informer, "The Making of Hollow Knight"](https://www.gameinformer.com/2018/10/16/the-making-of-hollow-knight)).
The article gives no reason for the creatures being insects; it notes that Metroidvania maps resemble ant nests.

**What follows visually.**
Hand-drawn insect and bug characters with a rule of "keep it simple"; the Infected Crossroads uses earlier enemies "mutated into deadlier, more-level appropriate variations" (same article).
Further examples were not verified.

**How creatures are generated.**
Team Cherry says they invent characters and areas they find interesting and then keep asking "what these bugs are thinking" (Nintendo Australia interview, seen only via a search summary: [link](https://www.nintendo.com.au/the-metamorphosis-of-hollow-knight-with-team-cherry-aussie-developer-interview)).
Not a generator in Martin's sense.

**Characters first or world first.**
Character first: the Knight began in a Ludum Dare 27 entry, Hungry Knight (2013); the kingdom idea came after a missed jam themed "Beneath the Surface", and "everything kinda snowballed from there" (Game Informer above).

## Avatar: The Last Airbender

**Single cause.**
I could not find the creators stating one in what I read.
The reliable structural fact is that the world is organised by four elements, with bending styles mapped to real martial arts (tai chi for water, hung gar for earth, northern Shaolin for fire, baguazhang for air), reported from Mental Floss and a fan wiki ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/entertainment/tv/avatar-the-last-airbender-tv-show-facts) and [Avatar Wiki](https://avatar.fandom.com/wiki/Influences_on_the_Avatar_franchise), both seen only through search summaries; secondary).

**What follows visually.**
The animals are described as hybrids of real species (turtle-ducks, badgermoles, a bison-manatee Appa; Konietzko "defined Appa as a bison-manatee hybrid" and studied both anatomies, per [Wikipedia on Appa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appa_(Avatar:_The_Last_Airbender)), secondary).
The live-action team says Appa and Momo are "not based on a single animal" ([Netflix Tudum](https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/avatar-the-last-airbender-creatures-appa-momo)), which is the remake's statement, not the original creators'.

**How creatures are generated.**
The "hybrid animals rule" is not stated by the creators in anything I could open.
In a Reactor interview the question about the animals was put to them and Konietzko answered "Read the book!", pointing to the art book, which I did not have ([Reactor interview](https://reactormag.com/interview-michael-dante-dimartino-and-bryan-konietzko-creators-of-avatar-the-last-airbender/)).
So "two real animals fused" is an observation of the results, not a quoted rule.

**Characters first or world first.**
Not verified.
DiMartino says that at the pitch they had "blocked out the three-season arc" (Reactor above), which suggests story structure before production, nothing more.

## Monster Hunter (Capcom)

**Single cause.**
A working rule, stated repeatedly: monsters are part of an ecosystem.
Tsujimoto: "In Monster Hunter, we always put a big focus on the ecology of monsters and other wildlife in the environments", and "When we're coming up with monster ideas, they need to make sense" ([Press Start interview, Wilds](https://press-start.com.au/features/2025/01/03/monster-hunter-wilds-interview)).
Fujioka: monsters are "designed with the world in mind and not in a vacuum" and should feel like "a convincing, cohesive part of the ecosystem" ([Vice](https://vice.com/en/article/the-developers-of-monster-hunter-explain-what-its-like-to-build-monsters)).

**What follows visually.**
A monster's body is built to fit what it does in a habitat.
Paolumu was built around the Coral Highlands' "rising air currents", asking which creature would use them, then "being airborne in a unique way": the team went from duckbills and elephant parts to things that inflate and ended on a hamster-like creature whose puffed cheeks keep it aloft ([Monster Hunter World monster-design interview, GameBanshee](http://www.gamebanshee.com/k4ak6)).
Banbaro's charge came from a designer idea of it "ripping up the map" with trees, and two sets of big antlers were added to support that (Vice above).
Constraint: monsters are built from "a number of base skeletal structures" so animations can be reused, which is why spiders are rare (Vice above).

**How creatures are generated.**
Described by the creators, in order: a framework of what kind of monster each stage needs; planners supply concept and area; artists develop the look and return attack ideas; the area's apex predator and herd roles fill the ecology ("there's always an Apex Predator in any given area") (Vice, Press Start, GameBanshee above).
Note that the first input in their account is the monster's job in the game's progression ("what kind of purpose does it serve and what job does it have", Tsujimoto), with ecology as the constraint on how it looks and behaves.
So the order is gameplay role, then habitat, then body.

**Characters first or world first.**
Areas and the game structure first, monster fitted into them; the world itself predates each new monster.

## Magic: The Gathering colour pie

**Single cause.**
Five colours, each a philosophy that wants something: "Green wants acceptance" ([Rosewater, It's Not Easy Being Green Revisited](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/its-not-easy-being-green-revisited-2015-08-10)); "Black wants power" ([In the Black Revisited](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/black-revisited-2015-07-27)).
The rule that makes it a generator: "In the color pie, flavor dictates function", the pie "clarifies each color's philosophy and then extends them to the game by bending mechanics around it", and "tells colors what they can do, it also tells them what they cannot" ([Rosewater, Value Pie, 2003](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/value-pie-2003-08-18-0)).
Rosewater also states the colour pie is "what the flavor and all the mechanics are built around" ([Council of Colors, 2016](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/council-colors-2016-08-22)).

**What follows visually.**
Per the columns, green is "more of a creature color than the others", with "access to larger and more vicious creatures"; black uses insects, spiders, vermin and undead, and its creatures "often live and thrive in the dark" (green and black columns above).
Colour decides what a creature does and is, not its anatomy: anatomy comes from the plane the card is set on (my inference; the columns I read do not say this).

**How creatures are generated.**
Stated: each colour has a distinct feel "in how the cards look and sound and in how the cards play", and breaking the pie is a recurring design problem (Value Pie above).
The repeatable rule in the creator's words: a card may do what its colour's philosophy allows and may not do what it forbids.
Rosewater says the pie "changes slightly over time" and "wasn't designed to change quickly or dramatically" ([Council of Colors](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/council-colors-2016-08-22) as summarised in search; wording not verified at the original).

**Characters first or world first.**
Not found; I have no source for whether the pie was written before the first creatures.
The pie as a design tool was articulated in columns from 2003 onward, long after the game launched in 1993 (the 1993 date is general knowledge, unsourced here).

## Spore's creature creator (Maxis)

**Single cause.**
None about a world.
The generator is technical: Wright described the editor as the "DNA template of a creature while the game, like a womb, builds the 'phenotypes'" ([Soren Johnson's Designer Notes, "Spore: My View of the Elephant"](https://www.designer-notes.com/spore-my-view-of-the-elephant/)).

**What follows visually.**
Procedural animation reads the body: leg count, leg length and symmetry give a "walking ratio", and anatomy also sets sounds, dance and social behaviour ([IEEE Spectrum, "Engineering Spore"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/engineering-spore)).
Wright: "We don't know what we're animating", so they tested "10 totally crazy, random creatures"; it "works for these seven, but for these two the legs look weird" (same article).

**How creatures are generated.**
By the player, from parts.
Limits the team set: looped-spine creatures were dropped because they caused too many animation exceptions, and data was cut so planets stay small, which "in effect curtail[s] a player's creativity" (IEEE Spectrum above).

**Where it produced sameness.**
In gameplay, per Soren Johnson: each part carries a "canned set of attributes", stats are the sum of parts, and "the player's creativity in designing the creature's shape has no impact on actual gameplay"; the editor gave an "infinite number of visually distinct creatures" but "the gameplay effects of the creature parts are unfortunately quite discrete" ([Designer Notes](https://www.designer-notes.com/spore-my-view-of-the-elephant/)).
Reader comments there report creatures that "just exchange blows" on an attack score (readers' reports, not the team).
I found no source from the team on visual sameness of creatures; that part of the question is not answered here.

**Characters first or world first.**
Not applicable: the tool came first, the creatures are the players'.

## Added: Dune (Frank Herbert)

**Single cause.**
Water as the controlling element of a planet-wide desert: from a 1957 assignment on dune control, Herbert saw "something enormously interesting going for me about the ecology of deserts" and asked "What if I had an entire planet that was desert?"; he set a planet where water "becomes the controlling element" (the law of the minimum), and assumed people there had evolved to survive it ([Herbert, as reproduced in Tim O'Reilly's Herbert biography, ch. 3](https://www.oreilly.com/tim/herbert/ch03.html)).
**Characters first or world first.**
World first: the ecology was the starting point.
The sandworm's immature stage trapping water and making the desert its mature stage needs is the book's ecology as narrated in that source, not a Herbert quote, so I treat it as secondary.
Visual and creature-generation sections are not filled: I did not verify creator statements on them.

## Patterns across them

Only what the cases above support.

- Most of the worlds were not generator-first. Pokémon had creatures first and added types mid-development; Hollow Knight had the character first and the kingdom after; Miyazaki says he picks the setting to fit the game's systems; the Mythos label was applied after the fact. The generator-first cases are Dune (ecology), Monster Hunter (habitat plus role, per each monster) and Magic's pie (designed as a rule set).
- The clearest generators constrain function or body, and say what is forbidden. Magic's pie says what a colour "cannot" do; Monster Hunter limits monsters to shared skeletons and to ecological sense; Spore's team dropped body shapes the animation could not handle; Dune's water scarcity limits every living thing.
- A cause that is only a mood or theme gives direction but not a test. Souls' themes (chaos, death, decay) work for demons, but Miyazaki's own method is briefs and image words, and the rest of the Souls look is not stated by the creators; Lovecraft's cause works as a negation (not human, not comprehensible) and was never a designer's rule.
- The weak cases lack a repeatable rule a second person could apply: Pokémon types were assigned "by fit", Avatar's hybrid rule is an observation I could not trace to the creators, and Spore's parts carried fixed stats unrelated to shape, so form made no difference to play.
- Withholding the world (Souls item text, Lovecraft's "cannot be described") is a delivery technique that sits on top of a generator; it did not replace one in any case.

## Human-like versus animal-like characters

Labels: **established** means repeated findings or a review; **single study** means one report I found; **lore** means designers or commentators saying it, without tested evidence.

- **Baby schema (Kindchenschema), the concept.** Lorenz proposed that infant features (large head, large eyes, protruding cheeks) act as an innate releaser of caretaking. Established as a concept in the literature, but a 2024 review (Kawaguchi and Waller) finds the term is used inconsistently and that cross-species continuity "has been assumed despite few empirical demonstrations", and says extreme sensitivity to it may be uniquely human ([Proc. R. Soc. B 2024, abstract via Europe PMC](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2024.0570)).
- **Baby schema raises cuteness and caregiving motivation in people.** Single study: Glocker et al. 2009 manipulated infant faces and found activation of the nucleus accumbens (reward and appetitive motivation) in nulliparous women, 'regardless of kinship' ([PNAS 2009, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0811620106)). Broadly accepted, but I found it as one fMRI study rather than a pooled estimate, and a meta-analysis was not located.
- **Cute images change behaviour.** Single study with an internal replication across three experiments: viewing puppies and kittens improved fine-motor and visual search performance and narrowed attention (Nittono et al. 2012, [PLoS One](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0046362)). A later free-throw study found the effect in men only and not under pressure ([Yoshikawa and Masaki 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.610817)), so treat it as fragile.
- **It applies to animal faces too.** Single study: three- to six-year-olds looked longer at the eyes of high-infantile dog, cat and human faces ([Borgi et al. 2014](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4019884/), seen via search summary). Kawaguchi and Waller's caution above applies.
- **Anthropomorphism.** Epley, Waytz and Cacioppo (2007, Psychological Review 114(4), 864-886) propose three determinants: how accessible and applicable human-based knowledge is (elicited agent knowledge), the motivation to understand and control things (effectance), and the desire for social connection (sociality); people anthropomorphise more when human-like cues are present, when they want to make sense of a thing, and when they lack social connection ([abstract summary via PubMed listing](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17907867/); I did not read the full paper). A widely cited theory with supporting experiments, not a design rule.
- **Human-like versus animal-like, directly.** I found no study comparing attachment to human-like versus animal-like fictional creatures. What exists separately: attachment to infant-like features (above) and a general tendency to read intent into nonhuman agents (above).
- **Uncanny valley.** Contested: a 2015 review found inconsistent evidence for the simple "almost human is unsettling" version and good support for perceptual mismatch (e.g. human and non-human cues clashing) ([Kätsyri et al. 2015, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00390/full), via search summary).
- **Eyes and expression.** Lore: animators treat eyes as the priority and say viewers fixate on a character's eyes ([Aaron Blaise via Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/art/character-design/disney-veteran-aaron-blaise-shows-how-to-create-animation-ready-characters); [SIGGRAPH](https://www.siggraph.org/?p=1931); both seen only through search summaries). Pokémon specifically: Sugimori describes learning to draw "more expressive faces, some dopey and some disgusting" ([Well Played, 2000 interview](https://www.well-played.com.au/pokemon-artist-ken-sugimori-talks-design-in-newly-translated-2000-interview/)); Compton's count of "angry anime eyes" on about a third of Gen 1 is one analyst's tally. McCloud's "masking" claim, that simple faces let readers project themselves onto a character, is lore from a book, disputed as universal ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masking_(comics))).
