# Bots

The bots have two jobs: the AI opponent people play against, and the pilots whose games produce balance data. Both jobs hit the same limit, which is judgement about the future.

## What the bots are for (Martin, 2026-10-17)

Opponents for people, nothing else. They never found a balance problem a person doesn't see within a couple of games of a deck, so bot-vs-bot numbers only rank candidates of the same cost; the measure of a bot is its ladder rating against humans, one per level and deck (`bot:<level>:<deck>` in the `ladder` table). Baseline, 17 October: the best pairing is Expert Cats at 1921, Martin and Kalista at 1870 beating the Expert three games in four, Laras at 2367. Goal: the best pairing at about 2100, a challenge, not a wall. Difficulty comes from real strength only, never a handicap.

The search scores a board after its own turn and the opponent's reply and never imagines its own next turn; that horizon, not evaluation detail, is why a practised player reaches 75% in a week (a Zebra played on turn one for one card). Levers, in order: depth (a second own turn in the search), decks that suit a bot's strengths, per-deck evaluation for ladder bots, the value network. Width on a one-turn search is not worth CPU (the 17 October budget runs: a few points at twice the cost, every interval across zero, stopped).

The candidate Expert (`referee:depth=1,den_sense=1,reply_turns=3,time_budget=8,w.pending_target=1.5,w.buried_presence=0.25`, what `AK_EXPERT_DEPTH=1 ./play` serves as the Expert): four changes on today's Expert. Two turns of sight: after the one-turn search ranks the roots, the best three are re-scored with the bot's own next turn (the reply, then its best `next_width` openings and a greedy finish), on the evaluation that values a creature already marked for removal and strength buried under covers. Den sense: every position after the opponent's reply carries the exact chance that they can capture the den from where they stand, computed over their public unseen pool (which cards would open the den from their connected chain, the odds their hand holds one), and that chance scores as a loss; the root screen keeps the strongest guard for every den-front crossroad. The reply as a whole turn: in the second stage the opponent's first action is chosen by their position after both actions (`reply_turns`), never one action at a time. Draw wins exact ties, so with one card in hand it draws before it plays. Against today's Expert on a frozen tree, 18 October, 20 games per opponent from both seats on all twelve decks (`results/bot_quality/wave2-v2`): every deck up, pooled +3.1 points over 1,320 paired games (95% interval +0.4 to +5.8), aristocrats +8 and food aggro +10 beyond noise, den-capture losses 441 to 377, mirrors 75/60/60 for the candidate; ladder seed with it as Expert (`results/ladder/seed_candidate.json`): Expert beats Normal 62.5% (today 60.8%). Cost on a quiet machine, forty turn-opening positions: 3.8 s a move on average and 9.2 s at worst against 1.8 and 7.5. What holds it back: the den chance multiplies the search's decisive value (1e9) while positional evaluation swings about 70 over a game, so a 10% capture chance outweighs any positional gain and the bot plays not to lose (more food losses, not only fewer den losses). A finite den loss alone fails the den puzzles, because sampled food wins still carry 1e9; the consistent fix is one finite value for every loss in the search, a character change that needs its own paired benchmark. Shipped as the Expert on 2026-10-20 (Martin: "of course it should be live if it makes the bot better"; `web/match.py` `EXPERT`, BOT_THINK_MAX raised to 20 s for its slowest moves); the ladder's Expert bots were re-seeded with it the same day (`web/ladder_seed.json` version 2026-10-20b: the Easy and Normal games kept, the 555 Expert pairs replayed at 10 games a pair, 16,860 games in all, anchored on the Easy and Normal bots so the Expert's gain shows as rating; Martin stopped it at 10 games a pair as the numbers had settled). ## The ladder

| Kind | What it does | Cost vs greedy | In the game UI |
|---|---|---|---|
| `random` | uniform legal moves; the floor | — | Easy |
| `greedy` | one-ply search over a hand-written board evaluation | 1× | Normal |
| `turn` | plans its complete current turn across sampled hidden worlds (determinization), no opponent reply | ~8–14× | Hard |
| `referee` | `turn` plus the opponent's reply turn, played by greedy in each sampled world | ~50–100× | Expert |
| `*_learned` | the same search driven by a learned linear evaluation (`data/learned/rung0.json`) | same as base | — |

All share one evaluation: `bots/greedy_bot.py` holds the terms and `bots/features.py` the feature code. Search knobs live at the top of `sim/runner.py`.

## Invariants

- **Generalist:** no deck slugs, archetype names or card ids in bot policy; deck knowledge comes from generic features (tags, keywords, geometry).
- **Honest:** bots never read the opponent's hand or deck order. Determinization samples hidden cards from public information only; regression tests enforce it.
- **Never nerf a card to fix a bot.** Every sim finding is either a card signal or a pilot flaw, and the two get different fixes.

## How far to trust them

- TurnBot beats greedy on every deck, and RefereeBot beats TurnBot on every deck by roughly 4 to 14 points. The gaps differ per deck, so a TurnBot matrix is directional: it underrates the decks it underplays (ramp, canine, egg) and overrates the ones it plays near-optimally.
- **The structural ceiling: no bot plans across turns.** The evaluation scores the current position. The `pending_payoff` term credits single delayed payoffs (hatching Eggs, bear timers), but no bot can play a multi-turn grow-then-win plan: a human piloting egg into cats won about 50% of games where the bots win about 24%. Treat any "loses" verdict for a scaling deck (egg, ramp) as unproven until a human has played it.
- **The human gap:** in Martin's reverse gauntlet (2026-09-29, 10 games a deck with each premade against the goodstuff pile piloted by RefereeBot) he won 50%, where RefereeBot piloting the same decks gets 26%; Egg 5 of 10 against the bots' 8%, Ramp 8 of 10 against 38%. The gap is the pilot, not the pile: the same bot pilots the pile in both.
- **The hand evaluation:** a hand is worth what its cards can do (`features.hand_value`): each card counts half its strength plus 1, the five best fully, the sixth half, every card 0.5 more; the opponent's hidden hand counts by its size only. It replaced a flat 3 points per card in hand plus 16 per live Roar, which made the bots hoard: RefereeBot spent 30-34% of its actions drawing where Martin spends 17-27%, and kept its Roar cards in hand. Paired against that evaluation (2026-10-15), RefereeBot gains on every deck, about +10 points on average (80 games a deck against Cats, Egg Control, Giants and Food Aggro: Food OTK +19, Aristocrats +16, Egg Control +16, Food Aggro +1), and GreedyBot at 200 games a matchup gains on 10 of 12 decks (Handlock +15; Food Aggro −1, Den Rush 0). RefereeBot's Handlock draws fell from 30% of its actions to 23%, and the Jerboa and Canine puzzles pass. A Roar card in hand earns nothing extra: the check for a live Roar runs only at the start of the bot's own turn, which the evaluation never sees. Still open: in a hand under five cards every draw fills a fully counted slot, so a small hand can look worth drawing into where placing is right.
- **RefereeBot's reply model is noisy (open, 2026-10-15):** it scores a move by playing out the opponent's reply in a few sampled worlds, and both halves of that are rough. The reply is played by the 1-ply greedy policy, which plays the same sampled hand well in one world and badly in the next; and a random effect (where Vultures land, which ally a Dawn effect eats) is rolled once per world. In Martin's Cockroach gauntlet the bot placed Jaguar where its only target was his Toad, losing Caracal and Jaguar to the Toad's ring: doing nothing scored about −62 because in its samples his second Sperm Whale wiped the Caracal and three Vultures landed on region corners, and the Jaguar line about 0 because there the Vultures happened to land elsewhere. Averaged over 30 samples the options tie within 2 points while single samples range from −87 to +36, so sharp positions are decided by noise. Fixes to weigh: more worlds for the reply (slower), rolling random effects several times or scoring their expectation, and a stronger reply policy than greedy. To see a decision: replay the game log to the action, build the bot's determinized worlds (`determinize`), and print each world's guessed hand, the reply `_advance` plays, and the eval terms at the end.
- **The mulligan is searched, not tabled (2026-10-21).** The bots kept every hand by default. A per-card table (`data/learned/opening_hand.json`, the opening-hand impact of each card over about 27k kept hands; `referee:mulligan=table`) was worth about zero, because ranking a hand's cards by a noisy table is ranking noise. Martin's rules for a good mulligan (a Python at 0 goes back, four commons are a weak hand, an Egg or a Black Bear is worth more now than later, two Hornets are one too many, a card that acts from the deck is dead in hand, a Honey Badger against a deck with nothing to target) are too many to write, and they all fall out of one thing: play the kept hand's first turns and look. `mulligan.search_choice` (`referee:mulligan=true`, any search bot) does that at each step of the mulligan: every option (keep, or send back one card) is played through two rounds of the opening in 24 sampled worlds (`determinize`: my deck's order and the opponent's hidden cards drawn afresh from their open list, the same worlds for every option), with the bot's own fast policy and evaluation, and the best is taken when it beats keeping by half a point. Under two seconds a hand. Tests: the Python goes back, a strong hand stays, four weak commons dig, the Bear stays. Paired benchmark on the frozen pool of 16:41, 20 games a deck against the same Expert keeping every hand (`results/bot_quality/wave2-mulligan`): +5.1 points a deck on average, interval +2.4 to +7.7, no deck down beyond its noise; Hoofed +18 and Colony +13 clear on their own, Canines, Fish and Food aggro +11 each just short of clear, the three piles flat at 83 to 84%. The gain sits where opening hands differ most in quality, the curve decks whose draws can strand their top end, and not in the piles, whose every hand is a keep. Den rush -5 within noise is the one to watch: a deck that wants any cheap hand gains nothing from digging. In the Expert since 2026-10-22. What it cannot see is a matchup's must-answer card on turn six: that is matchup knowledge, to be read from a logged corpus of every matchup played many times.
- **Known blind spots:** the region-control term overvalues the middle row, so bots never contest the flank rows as a den-rush lane. Cats beat aggro about 87% in sims, while Martin's play went 2 of 3 to aggro: the bots dump their hand where they should hold disruption. That matchup is a pilot flaw, not a card problem.

## The learned evaluator

Self-play TD(λ) (`learn/td.py`, `learn/train.py`) fits the weights of a linear evaluation.

- **rung-0** learns weights for the 11 hand-written terms. It beats the hand weights on 6 of 7 decks and is the shipped learned eval (`rung0.json`); `rung0_identity.json` reproduces the hand weights.
- **rung-1** added 13 dynamics features and regressed on 5 of 7 decks (food_otk −20 points). Cause: several new features duplicate rung-0 terms, and an unregularized fit on raw features moved weight toward self-play's majority rush dynamic. Training converged, so it wasn't undertrained.
- A better evaluation alone may not close the planning gap: the egg-vs-cats gap may need search depth plus a good leaf evaluation (the horizon problem quiescence search and extensions solve in chess). The search's pruning has its own horizon bug: it ranks a candidate by its half-resolved position, before its own sub-choice (a target, a kept card, an extra placement) resolves, so Owl never reached RefereeBot's search in any of the 18 turns Martin played it. `quiesce=true` resolves those sub-choices greedily before ranking and is part of the Expert since 2026-10-21 (below, under the open misplays).

Train with `.venv/bin/python -m animal_kingdom.learn.train --out results/learn/<run>` and promote with `animal_kingdom.learn.promote`.

- **Outcome-fitted linear (2026-09-30):** a logistic fit of the rung-1 features on the outcomes of 5,600 RefereeBot games predicts winners better than the hand eval (held-out log-loss 0.570 against 0.591) but loses Aggro and Canine by 12-13 points as a pilot. Unconstrained, it credits what merely correlates with winning (fewer cards left in deck, live Roars in hand), which a search then exploits; sign constraints and dropping those terms remove the exploit but also the readiness term's den-race value.

## The value network

A small network over the rung-2 features (rung 1 plus hand and board context: Roars in hand against live ones, hand strength, fliers, affordable food cards, hand cards sharing a tag with the board, engines on the board, extra actions coming, den-front defence), trained on who won self-play games and iterated: train, let TurnBot play the next round with it, retrain.

- `learn/selfplay.py` plays TurnBot games (hand eval or a network) across all 36 pairings of the premades and the pile, 5% random moves, and records the positions the search scores; about 4,000 games (150k positions) in 40 minutes on 8 cores.
- `learn/net.py` fits one hidden ReLU layer on outcomes (split by game, a linear fit alongside as the yardstick) and writes a `NetEval` artifact (`bots/net_eval.py`, stdlib inference, scaled into hand-eval points). Load it as any learned eval: `referee_learned:eval=<path>`.

## Open misplays

- **A placement is ranked before its own sub-choice resolves (Martin's gauntlet game QR4OFW-4, 2026-10-21).** The Expert played a Hornet in the middle to kill a Badger with a Black Bear in reach: its root screen scored the Hornet placement at the half-resolved position, before the target was picked, so the three roots that went on to the reply search were chosen on positions no Hornet target had shaped. `quiesce=true` resolves a placement's sub-choices greedily before the screen ranks it. Paired benchmark on the frozen pool of 16:41, 20 games a deck against the shipped Expert (`results/bot_quality/wave2-quiesce`): +4.3 points a deck on average, interval +3.0 to +5.6, no deck down beyond its noise; Hoofed +13, Colony +12, Handlock +11 and Giants +8 clear on their own, the three piles flat at 82 to 88% where there is little left to win. The gain is not where the sub-choice density is (Handlock has the most choices a placement, Giants the fewest, both gained): it sits in the decks that were losing, which is where a better-chosen root changes the result. In the Expert since 2026-10-21.
- **A cut-off piece in the den front is a threat (Martin's game GMIDKA, 2026-10-21).** The threat term counted only enemy front pieces connected to their den; two Falcons flown in cut off read as zero, and the Advanced bot, which has no reply search, spent two turns at the other end of the board until a connector landed and the den fell. `features.hq_threat_terms(..., reach)` adds `hq_threat_reach` of a threat for a front piece one empty crossroad from the enemy's chain (`GreedyWeights.hq_threat_reach`); puzzle `falcons_in_the_front`. Paired benchmark (`results/bot_quality/wave2-reach/`): the Normal pilot +1.0 on average at 0.5 with no deck below noise, -0.4 at 1.0; the Expert -0.8 at 0.5 (Fish -10), since its reply search already plays the capture out. So the Normal pilot ships with 0.5 (`web.match.NORMAL`) and the Expert without.
- **A food lead counts as winning (Martin, 2026-10-20).** The hand evaluation values food at one point per net food plus `food_proximity` (30) times the fraction of the way to the win, linearly, while a strength point on the board is half a point and a den-front crossroad forty: a 60-food lead scores like 156 points of strength. Martin's games against the Expert on the live server since the 17 October release (`deploy/deploy.sh pull`, 64 games, he won 46) replayed with the Expert's own evaluation watching from the bot's side: in 22 of his 35 wins the bot believed it was ahead at some turn, and in 15 of those the lead was the two food terms alone, with board presence and region control negative. The board part of the evaluation on its own separates his wins from the bot's (median -73 against +13 at the bot's last turn); the food terms are what break it. Martin's read of the eight positions where the bot lost from "ahead": a food lead you cannot convert is worth nothing, the board is what converts, through regions into food and through the den front into the capture. The September self-play fit (rung-0) had already put the net food weight near zero and the proximity weight negative. `GreedyWeights.food_curve` (an exponent on the proximity) and `food_race` (mine minus theirs) are the dials; defaults are the shipped linear terms. Rescored offline on the same 47 games, a cubed curve takes mid-game agreement with the result from 30 to 38 of 47 and the lost-from-ahead count from 8 to 0, which is a fit on the games that found it. The paired benchmark (`results/bot_quality/wave2-food/`, 15 pool decks, 60 games a deck) on two shapes: the cubed curve with a quarter of the net food term +1.4 on average, the cubed race between both sides' food with no net-food term +2.7 (Handlock +14, Cats +12, Canines +9, Thick Skin +8; Small Fry -12.5, Fish -7, Food Aggro -5: the decks whose plan is the food lead the curve discounts, so food's worth should one day depend on the deck's power to convert it). The race shape is the Expert since 2026-10-21 (`web.match.EXPERT`: `w.food_progress=0,w.food_curve=3,w.food_proximity=60,w.food_race=true`); his next games are the test that counts. The diagnosis tools are scratch scripts from that day (belief curves per game, the evaluation split into terms, an offline rescore); the page he read is the artifact "Where the Expert Lost". The same night the search given more width (`deepen_roots=6, deepen_top=6, next_width=5`, `results/bot_quality/wave2-wider/`) came out at -0.5 points on average over the 15 pool decks and 1.5 times slower: width does nothing while the evaluation is wrong about what wins.
- **Taipan marks the wrong animal** (puzzle `test_taipan_marks_the_python`, from Martin's game of 2026-09-30): with Wolf on 4,2 opening 3,2, Taipan there must mark his Python (8). Diagnosed 2026-10-17 as two blindnesses, not one: the hand eval's `pending_payoff` pays the same for any delayed effect (so the mark is worth the same on a Raven), and `board_presence` reads top units only, so covering the Python with the Taipan scored like removing it, and the bot covered it. The candidate `TARGET_AWARE_WEIGHTS` (`pending_target` 1.5 per point of strength a scheduled removal will take, `buried_presence` 0.25 per point of strength under a cover) marks the Python in 5 of 5 seeds; either term alone fails. Both are rung-2 features too (`pending_target_strength`, `buried_presence`), so round 4 of the network trains on them. The candidate is off by default until the paired benchmark against the old weights passes (`results/bot_quality/wave2-target-eval-*`); then it becomes the default and the puzzle drops the explicit weights. Still to do: retrain round 4; prove it on current cards with a 7x7 at 30 games per opponent (each deck's result lands as it finishes). Re-seed the ladder's bot ratings only when a bot deck joins the pool or a pilot changes materially (more than about ten points on average in the paired benchmark, as the 2026-10-20 Expert's +36); a smaller change is below the seed's own noise at ten games a pair and the ladder absorbs it, since the bots are rated players and every game against a person moves them (Martin, 2026-10-21: not a full re-seed after every little change). The recipe: run `.venv/bin/python -m animal_kingdom.sim.ladder_seed --games N --jobs 7 --out results/ladder/seed_<date>.json --anchor animal_kingdom/web/ladder_seed.json` for N of 2, 4, 6 and 8 in turn (each pass resumes from the games file, so a stop leaves every pair with the same number of games; the anchor puts the new numbers on the scale people were rated on; about four hours for 15 decks), then copy the output to `animal_kingdom/web/ladder_seed.json` with a new `version`; the server applies it once at startup and resets every bot to its seeded rating.

- **The Expert covered and then ate its own Snake Egg (Martin's HYWCZW, 2026-10-22).** Two causes, both candidates, neither shipped. The pending-payoff term kept crediting an egg's hatch after the egg was buried (a suspended timer) or eaten (a cancelled one), because the engine clears those entries only at the owner's next turn start: `w.pending_alive=1` counts a scheduled effect only while its animal is on top of its crossroad. And the City Spider placed beside its own Black Bear: the one-turn stage saw the Bear die at Dawn and still ranked the Spider's middle square 9 points above drawing, because a 5-strength Bear is 2.5 points and two cards 1.4 (the price list, not the horizon); `deepen_chase=true` keeps the second stage deepening while the best deepened root trails the next one-turn score, which makes it draw there. Paired benchmark of the two together (`results/bot_quality/wave2-eggchase`, the first on the Rust bots): +0.6 points a deck, interval -1.4 to +2.5, Tempo -8.3 beyond its noise, nothing up beyond its noise; it does not ship. Bots do not exploit an egg that cannot hatch, so the fix shows nowhere against bots, and chase costs about 1.5x the move. What the position really showed is the price list: a Bear and two cards worth less than one turn of a middle crossroad, which is the learned evaluator's job, not another dial's.

## Throughput

The complete-turn search is bushy, not deep: turn trees are only 2 to 6 plies but explode in breadth on combo decks. The shipped levers are a node budget (`TURN_MAX_SEARCH_NODES=80`, byte-identical play on all decks) and collapsing Owl/Raven's keep-or-shuffle choices to the top 2 in lookahead. food_otk remains the slowest deck, about 13× greedy.

**Engine speed (2026-10-20).** The Expert's move is an engine number, not a search number: the search clones a position, applies an action and evaluates for every move it imagines (about twenty thousand positions a real move), so the per-position cost of `state.clone`, `rules.apply_action` and `features.evaluate` is the whole bill. Measured with a sampling profile (`tools/engine_speed/sample_moves.py`; cProfile's per-call overhead inflated the small functions three-fold and pointed at the wrong lines), then changed with identical play as the acceptance test: `tools/engine_speed/record.py` records 22 games of Easy, Normal and the Expert (the Expert without its time budget, so the machine's load cannot change its moves) and `compare.py` checks them move for move (the recordings live in `results/engine_speed/`, the baseline from commit 612b1fbd). The pass took the 22 games from 217 to 135 seconds, every move the same: a card without an anthem no longer walks the board to learn it has none, and a board evaluation gathers the aura sources once (`strength.aura_sources`) instead of per unit; a clone takes its parent's random-stream snapshot and becomes a generator only when it draws (`state.Rng`: `getstate` built a 625-int tuple per clone, a tenth of a move); the plain-data copy tests leaves first; hook wrappers are built once per hook; the connection search and the region fractions read the adjacency and the board directly. PyPy 3.12 was tried and is slower than CPython 3.14 here (13.3 s against 8.9 s on the same eight moves). What remains is flat: nothing above 7 percent of a move, the largest the connection search, the region fractions and the hook lookups. On top of that the server runs the engine compiled (mypyc, 2026-10-20, Martin: "no downside, go for it"): `deploy/Dockerfile` compiles the engine (state, rules, effects, strength, statics, maps) and the search (the evaluator, the greedy, turn and referee bots, determinize) to C extensions in a build stage, then plays the 22 recorded games on them and compares every move with `tools/engine_speed/baseline.json`; a compiled engine that plays differently never deploys. Same moves, 1.9 times faster on the eight-move yardstick (3.3 s against 6.4 s; the engine alone gave 1.5). The tree stays pure Python, so an edit on localhost is never shadowed by a stale compiled module. What it took in the source: `GameState`, `Rng` and `Result` marked plain classes for mypyc (they are built by `__new__`), a few annotations mypy had inferred too narrowly, a dead block after a return in `referee_bot._reply_opening` removed, `reversed()` over a plain list written as a slice (mypyc crashed on it), and `rules._decided`: a rule that asks "is the game over" twice around a call that can win it must ask afresh, because mypy keeps its first answer across the call and mypyc checks that narrowed type at run time. Re-record the baseline whenever play legitimately changes (a bot, a card, the Expert spec). The next factor comes from the search doing less, which changes play and needs the paired benchmark, not from the engine.

Untried: a transposition cache keyed on the existing hidden-info-safe position digest (`TurnSearcher._observation_key`), since placing A then B reaches the same board as B then A. Also killer-move ordering in the turn beam.

## The engine in Rust (2026-10-23)

Martin's goal (2026-10-22): bots trained on the game's principles rather than its cards, patch-proof, with no cloud compute, and an engine fast enough that self-play rounds take minutes on his laptop. The pure-Python engine ran an Expert move in about 3 s, 60% of it in `apply_action`; the search and the evaluation take the rest, so only a port of all three pays. `engine_rs/` (a PyO3 module, `ak_engine`; `engine_rs/README.md` has the build and the interface) holds the engine, every card and the bots in Rust, with the Python package as the specification: `AK_RUST_BOTS=1` makes `sim.runner.make_bot` return a `RustBot` for every kind, and the benchmark, self-play and the sims take it from there. The proof is four oracles under `tools/engine_rs/`: every logged game replays identically through both engines (143 games on today's rules, 1,531 more agree up to where today's rules break them), random games over every card agree on every position and legal-action list (700 games the first night), the Expert, Normal and Easy choose the same move with the same root scores on the same positions and seeds (60 positions), and the rung-2 features and the evaluation agree to 1e-9 (300 positions). Speed the first night, measured by `engine_rs/target/release/bench` on a busy machine: the Expert about 25 times the Python one (a 4,080-game self-play round in 51 minutes on seven cores, where the Python run managed 2,800 games in five and a half hours; a 15-deck paired benchmark in about 35 minutes). A second pass the next day, from a profile of the move (half of it was copying and allocation: the search copies the position about 14,000 times an action, once for every line it imagines): mimalloc as the allocator, the random generator and the scheduled timers behind copy-on-write handles, the board's containers cloned by memcpy instead of element by element, the turn flags' lists inline, no event maps built for imagined placements. About 30% off the move under load; the bot oracle, which times both engines on the same positions, read the network Expert at 57x and the hand Expert at 41x the Python bots. The afternoon added the board's stacks and the turn flags cloned by memcpy, a 24-byte unit record and a profile-guided build of the local module (`engine_rs/pgo.sh`, 9% on its own): 24.4 ms an Expert action at the start of the day, 15.7 ms at the end on the timing binary; the bot oracle reads the network Expert at 63x and the hand Expert at 47x the Python bots. Three things were tried, measured slower and dropped (`engine_rs/README.md`). What remains is structural (a copy per evaluated candidate, in both engines), worth about 15% for a rewrite of every mutation in the effect system. The live server still runs the Python bots: the Rust engine joins the image only when Martin says so, and nothing in `deploy/` reads `AK_RUST_BOTS`.

The rule from here: a change to the Python engine or bots is mirrored in `engine_rs` and reproven by the oracles before any run uses it; a change to a card is data and needs nothing.

The live server thinks on it since 2026-10-23: the Fly image (`deploy/Dockerfile`) builds the wheel in its own stage, runs the fuzz oracle against the Linux build and then the fast test tier on the Rust bots, and sets `AK_RUST_BOTS=1`; the Python engine still runs the match itself, and a game with a card the Rust table lacks (a test's fixture table) falls back to the Python bot of the same spec. The Rust move releases the GIL, so the server keeps serving while a bot thinks. Two lessons from the first day. The oracle tools put their own repo root first on `sys.path`: the venv's editable install points at the main tree, so run from another worktree they were testing that tree's Python against this tree's Rust. And float noise is not equivalence: two roots whose lines tie within 1e-9 were deepened in opposite orders by the two engines (the arithmetic rounds differently), which shifts the shared random stream and changed one root's score by a fifth of a point in one of 60 positions; both engines now order such ties by candidate order (a rounded sort key), and the bot oracle covers the network Expert and the hand Expert.

## Measuring a bot change

The procedure is the `bot-change` skill (`.claude/skills/bot-change/SKILL.md`): `tools/bot_bench/bench.sh run <name> "+dial=value"` runs the paired benchmark (`sim.bot_comparison`) on a frozen checkout at origin, candidate and baseline being the shipped Expert untimed with and without the change, against the shipped Expert; `report` reads it; a result ships into the level's spec when the pooled mean is up, no deck is down beyond its interval and the gain lands where the change's mechanism predicts. The run keeps its game logs under `results/bot_quality/<name>/logs/`. Mirror self-play is too noisy for small pilot changes; always compare against a fixed opponent.

Freeze the misplayed position as a puzzle in `tests/test_bot_puzzles.py` before fixing it. The strongest puzzles are real positions from bot games that Martin has judged: saved as `GameState` snapshots in `tests/puzzles/`, shown to him in the client's lab view (`#/lab/<name>`, card text on hover), asserting the set of moves he accepts (a guard puzzle asserts a move the bot already gets right). Puzzles catch specific misplays but don't prove strength: readiness at 0 passes all of them and still loses games.
