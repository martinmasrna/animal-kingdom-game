# How Riot builds regions and derives champions and Legends of Runeterra cards from them

Scope and honesty note. Riot's region documents themselves (the internal "region bibles") are not public. No source I could reach shows one, gives its length, or lists its headings. What is public is the practice around them: the 2014 reboot post, naming and champion-making articles, narrative team posts, and Legends of Runeterra (LoR) design interviews. Several Riot pages (the Nexus articles on the 2017 and 2018 narrative work, the full "Exploring Runeterra" post) now redirect to the League homepage, so I read them through mirrors and summaries; where a claim rests on a search-engine snippet or a mirror, the text says so. "Source says" and "I infer" are marked.

## 1. The reboot: why the Institute of War went

Source says: in 2014 Riot declared that the Institute of War, the Fields of Justice, Summoners and the League are no longer canon; players are still called summoners in-game, but not in the story. The reasons, as summarised in a [collection of the red posts](https://www.surrenderat20.net/2014/09/red-post-collection-exploring-runeterra.html): the original framework "fostered creative stagnation"; all-powerful Summoners made champions "little more than puppets"; and some game logic does not survive being turned into story (Tommy Gnox: "Garen probably wouldn't beat up on his sister"). Riot wanted room for conflict that the framing could not hold, such as espionage, diplomacy and cross-regional war. Gnox's guiding rule was to "do no harm, and respect core themes." The same post says the maps will always be "evocative of actual places within the world." A [Scribd copy of the original dev blog](https://www.scribd.com/document/276236455/Developer-s-Blog), as summarised, adds that Riot would keep champion identities consistent while exploring Runeterra across several formats and would ask players which regions they wanted.

Mechanism, as I read it: the old lore was a justification layer. Every champion needed a reason to be in the League, so every story started from the same room and ended in the same fight. The game's needs (a roster, a match) were the world's only shape. The reboot reversed the dependency: the world exists first and champions are people from somewhere who also appear in the game. A search summary of [Riot's 2018 post](https://www.surrenderat20.net/2018/02/red-post-collection-quick-gameplay_16.html) states the target as building Runeterra into a world that can grow "and give each champion a distinct place."

What the new foundation consisted of is thinner than the "region bible" story suggests. The 2014 post names no documents or pillars, as far as the summaries show. The world had regions and factions already scattered through champion bios; the reboot removed the frame and asked champions to be read as inhabitants of those places. The consolidated apparatus (Universe site, interactive map, lore review) came later, in 2016 to 2018, and the 2018 post says the lore had until then been handled "somewhat haphazardly by enthusiastic people from across many different teams." Ariel Lawrence and Laurie Goulding began reviewing all new lore at the end of 2017 so the pieces fit; the post says the team treats inconsistencies "like bugs" (summary of the [2018 post](https://www.surrenderat20.net/2018/02/red-post-collection-quick-gameplay_16.html)). I infer that the order matters for us: Riot got regions as culture first, and the bible-like machinery only after years of damage from not having it.

## 2. The region as a unit

What I could establish. In the 2019 narrative post ([Riot, dev post "Narrative in 2019"](https://www.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/dev/dev-narrative-in-2019)), the team says that when the map was charted they focused on Valoran, Shurima and Ionia and deliberately left territories between them unclaimed. A region is therefore, in Riot's practice, a place on a shared map plus the peoples in it plus the history that connects it to its neighbours. The interactive map in 2018 fixed how places physically relate. LoR then flattened each region into a one-line identity. The support page ([Regions of Runeterra](https://support.riotgames.com/en-us/legends-of-runeterra/gameplay/regions-of-runeterra)) gives taglines: Demacia "Stand Together", Noxus "All-Out Assault", Ionia "Swift and Sure", Freljord "Frozen Fortitude", Bilgewater "Risk and Reward", Targon "Align the Stars", Shurima "Awaken Your Empire", Bandle City "Explore and Unite", Shadow Isles "Death and Sacrifice", Piltover and Zaun "Machinations and Mayhem".

What I could not establish: document structure, length, who writes it, who must read it. The closest primary hint is how champions are made (section 4): a three-person pod with an informal exchange of artifacts, not a handed-down spec. Consistency across media is attempted by review, not by a single document: the lore review of 2018, and in 2024 an announcement by Laura DeYoung, head of IP Creative, that Arcane becomes canon and that "getting to that consistent state is not going to be a sudden flip of a switch" (reported in [GosuGamers](https://www.gosugamers.net/news/69707-arcane-becomes-canon-in-league-of-legends-as-riot-overhauls-lore)). That report names the problem: years of short stories, events, cinematics and spin-offs, with Hextech's origin as the example, plus "Legends of Runeterra" among the spin-offs that added conflicts.

Inference: a region at Riot is defined less by a fixed list of attributes than by a handful of champions who embody it. LoR's design director says so directly, see section 5.

## 3. Names

Source says, in Riot's [2017 "Naming New Champions" article](https://www.surrenderat20.net/2017/12/red-post-collection-battlecast-illaoi_26.html?m=1) (read via the red-post collection, as the Nexus original redirects):

- Narrative writers are the primary namers. Naming starts once the art, fantasy and personality direction is set, and writers test names inside short story sketches.
- Each champion gets three to five ranked goals for the name. Camille's included evoking aristocratic eccentricity and sounding attractive without sounding beautiful.
- General checks apply to every name: does the shortened form still work, is it easy to pronounce across localisations (shoutcasters are the test), is it pronounced as it looks, does it feel good to say, does it resemble another champion's name.
- Namestorms run 100 to 200 candidates, sometimes over 500 counting spelling variants. They narrow to 25 to 100, then to about ten for localisation review, where each gets Yes, Maybe or Absolutely Not. Localisation can leave two or three. The three-person "DNA group" (designer, writer, concept artist) decides, and if they disagree the writer usually wins.
- Sources are not baby name books alone. Illaoi's name was built from patterns across Polynesian, Australian, Greek, Hawaiian and Latin naming, with double L used and V avoided, because she comes from a distant island nation. Ornn's came from volcano and Norse reading.
- Letters carry tone: "K" feels punchy (Kayn), "R" suave (Rakan), "I" exotic and un-yordle-like. Q, R and L are avoided where pronunciation varies.
- Conventions exist per culture, stated in the article but not as a document: Targonian names sound Hellenic, Void names resemble ancient Shuriman with an apostrophe between two parts, yordle names use double letters and are friendly and simple (Heimerdinger is the exception). I did not find written conventions for Demacia, Noxus or Freljord; the wide claim that Riot keeps regional "linguistic rules" comes only from a name-generator site and is unverified.
- Failures: Xayah was "Xaya", a medical term for testicles in Azerbaijani, caught late. Camille sounded like "camel" in some languages. Aurelion Sol was once "Heaven's Coil" as a deliberately bad placeholder. Temp names are avoided because "temp names have a tendency to take over" (Lawrence, as quoted in a search summary of the article); writers use unpronounceable or ridiculous working names so nobody gets attached. Taliyah's gameplay working name Stoneweaver became her title.

Mechanism: the name is judged against goals written first, and the long list is there because the first good-looking names are the category average. Rejecting is cheap, and a list of 100+ lets writers find what the favourites have in common and regenerate (the article says exactly that). For LoR followers I found no account of how they were named; that is a gap.

## 4. Champion from region: four cases

Source says the working unit is a pod of a core game designer, a narrative writer and a concept artist, per Matthew Dunn in an [interview about Kindred](https://tribecafilm.com/news/kindred-champion-riot-league-of-legends-charachter-design-mathew-dunn-riot-games). The pod picks a gameplay role first, brainstorms in the open, an artist's sketch (a lamb in a wolf mask shooting a wolf in a lamb mask) steered the team to death as the theme, then design built paper kits, artists drew, and writers wrote 12 to 14 "story sketches" Dunn calls "our version of concept art," mostly for internal inspiration. Two to three months went on voice and script after that. The first document is not a pitch; it is a gameplay role, a sketch and a story sketch.

- Kindred: role first (a marksman who also takes jungle objectives), art idea second, theme from an image, story sketches in parallel with the kit. (Dunn, above.)
- Kalista, from Riot's [Champion Insights as collected on Surrender at 20](https://www.surrenderat20.net/2014/12/red-post-collection-kalista-champion.html): concept art came first (an undead female spear thrower, codename Spectral Legionnaire). The narrative writer joined early ("We sort of knew she was betrayed and that was like a quarter of who she was") and wrote several drafts to see which resonated. The designer was prototyping cooperative mechanics, and the writer said it was "a good example of the background and the story getting inspired by the gameplay direction", producing The Pledge. Reviewers thought she looked like a mindless zombie, so the face was reworked; the plural voice ("We are the Spear of Vengeance") was fixed during voice-over.
- Yone: described by Riot staff as designed top-down from the character, with the pod's "early meetings explore who the champion could be" ([thread of the Champion Insights post](https://devtrackers.gg/leagueoflegends/p/7dd93904-champion-insights-yone); the original article was not retrievable, and the "top-down" claim comes from a commenter, not Riot).
- Ixtal, region from champions: per the [2019 narrative post](https://www.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/dev/dev-narrative-in-2019), an unclaimed patch of the map became Ixtal through three champions: Ezreal as explorer asking who lived there, Neeko showing present peoples trying to settle, and Qiyana supplying the culture (a people who outlasted Azir's empire). Sylas was created to complicate Demacia, "often portrayed as simply good compared to Noxus," by exploring a culture oppressing its own magic.

Mechanism and what it says: the order varies (kit, art or character first), and Riot does not claim otherwise. What is constant is a loop of cheap artifacts (sketch, story sketch, paper kit) that let the three disciplines react to each other before anything is expensive. The region constrains by being the place the story sketch must fit. And in the Ixtal case the direction reverses: the region's identity was written through champions, which suggests a bible can be built partly from the people in it.

## 5. Legends of Runeterra: regions as mechanical identities

Source says. Region is LoR's deck-building frame. Design director Andrew Yip says the team "divided all the cards into different regions" and picked the initial champions to "reflect the common characteristics we think should be true for many of the cards from that region"; his Freljord example: effects "driven by the champions themselves", Ashe frostbite, Tryndamere never dies, Braun a big shield ([Geek Culture interview](https://geekculture.co/geek-interview-building-decks-in-legends-of-runeterra-from-the-ground-up-with-design-director-andrew-yip/)). Dillon Buckner says regions were chosen for lore depth ("strange omissions" otherwise) and for gameplay potential, and that the game is "League-inspired / themed rather than an attempt to more literally translate gameplay" ([Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/10365/legends-of-runeterra-developer-dillon-buckner-talks-about-the-games-early-development-designing-its-unique-economy-and-riots-approach-to-card-balance)). Steve Rubin says champions are not one-to-one copies of their League versions: the team designs around what players love, "mechanics" for Yasuo, "theme" for Vladimir ([Esports News UK](https://esports-news.co.uk/2019/10/19/steve-rubin-legends-runeterra-riot-interview/)).

How a region's identity became keywords, with two cases where designers describe it. Shurima: design lead Mark Sassenrath built it on three themes (time and inevitability, glory of the ascended, grandeur and brutality of the desert); the Buried Sun Disc exists because "the Targon set introduced Landmarks as a card type and, in Shurima lore, the Sun Disc is such an iconic landmark"; narrative lead Mel Lei tied it to the three champions who can ascend in League ([Inven Global](http://www.invenglobal.com/articles/13420/time-glory-brutality-how-the-legends-of-the-runeterra-team-brought-shurima-to-life)). Targon: the team "laid out our design pillars very early", three of them (cosmic power, day and night, the journey up the mountain); Celestials are summoned through Invoke so their cost is built in; Daybreak and Nightfall came from day and night; support bonuses were made permanent because Targon is full of support champions; and the lead designer says they want a deck for every two-region pairing and seed connections to get it ([Reimaru Files summary of the design dive](https://www.reimarufiles.com/2020/09/10/legends-of-runeterras-design-dive-on-call-of-the-mountain/)). Note a pairing detail: Trundle's trolls were first built around zero power and regeneration, which did not work; the idea that fit both Freljord and Targon was that trolls want expensive cards.

I did not find designer statements on Demacia, Noxus or Ionia mechanics; the mapping (Demacia "Stand Together" and so on) is visible only in the taglines above. I infer the pattern is: one word for the region's temperament from lore, three to four pillars, then keywords that behave like the theme (Invoke is a pilgrimage, Plunder rewards hitting the enemy). Inference, not a stated rule.

The narrative team inside LoR is local: it writes voice lines, flavour text and character interactions, trades assets with Riot's global narrative team, and creates characters of its own (Rubin: Tianna Crownguard, Lux's aunt, built to explore Demacia's anti-magic stance and later used in League). Rubin says followers are a way to "flesh out Runeterra." A developer interview noted a lapse where flavour text was missing at first ("an oversight on our part as we were learning how to be part of this new process", per a search summary).

What LoR added to the world quickly: Bandle City, whose description is "everywhere and nowhere" (Game Director Yip, via [Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/legendsofruneterra/articles/14655/legends-of-runeterra-new-region-bandle-city-beyond-the-bandlewood-expansion)), rested on existing yordle lore plus portals; Bandlewoods are a game device. The same piece says champions from regions not in the game, such as the Void, are placed in an existing locale that fits their lore. A separate interview recap says Ionia holds about 13 of the 20 most popular champions and the team once considered a fictional Ionian civil war to split them ([Out of Games](https://outof.games/news/2908-legends-of-runeterra-will-only-have-10-regions-balance-patches-dev-more-riot-dovagedys-dev-interview-recap/)). That is the clearest sign that the world was bent where the card game needed it. No source I reached says the world bible was "too thin or too thick" for LoR in those words.

## 6. Failure and criticism

- Lore lived in many hands. The 2018 post itself admits "haphazard" handling and promises to treat inconsistencies as bugs. The 2024 canon announcement admits years of retcons, with spin-offs (including LoR) adding conflicts, and treats consistency as a gradual repair ([GosuGamers](https://www.gosugamers.net/news/69707-arcane-becomes-canon-in-league-of-legends-as-riot-overhauls-lore); see also the [PC Gamer report](https://www.pcgamer.com/arcane-is-officially-canon-and-from-now-on-league-of-legends-lore-is-going-to-stop-contradicting-itself-all-over-the-place-say-riot/), whose body was not retrievable). A bible does not prevent drift when many teams and media write against it.
- Rebuilding is expensive. The 2018 post says full champion updates take too long to cover the whole setting, so lore updates are decoupled from gameplay and delivered by comics, videos and stories.
- Regions that thin out. Ixtal exists because of three champions and a gap in the map; the LoR team did not turn it into a region, and an interview recap leaves it unconfirmed ([Out of Games](https://outof.games/news/2908-legends-of-runeterra-will-only-have-10-regions-balance-patches-dev-more-riot-dovagedys-dev-interview-recap/)). I infer a region needs enough champions to give a card game a pool; depth alone did not suffice.
- Player reception. A search summary reports PC Gamer's Steven Messner calling the reboot "dumb" and others praising later steps; this is not a primary source.
- The reboot and later work contradicted each other. Arcane's Hextech origin conflicted with the Brackern lore, and Viktor's fate is not definitively known even to the team, per Riot's Paul Bellezza in a Polygon interview (via a search summary).
- Keeping the world small enough to use: I found no Riot statement that says so. What they do instead is limit LoR to ten regions, anchor each to a few champions, and place unmatched champions in existing regions.

## What transfers to Animal Kingdom and what does not

- Transfers: a society defined by who lives where and what it values, not just geography, because Riot's regions work as culture and temperament, and the taglines show a one-line identity is enough to drive a card pool.
- Transfers: a one-word temperament per society plus three or four pillars, as in Targon's pillars and Shurima's three themes, because they give designers a test for any card (does it belong?). This is what the unnamed legendaries lack.
- Transfers: name goals written before names, a long list, and a cull, because Riot's namestorms of 100 to 200 are how they avoid the average. Our deck-design skill already names the character first; add three to five ranked goals per name, derived from the society's page.
- Transfers: a cheap loop of artifacts (story sketch, paper kit, sketch) before anything is final, because Riot's pods find the character that way and the world can be written through the individuals in it, as Ixtal was through Ezreal, Neeko and Qiyana.
- Transfers: champions chosen to carry a region's traits, as Yip describes, because our legendaries are the same device. Write each society page by asking which legendary embodies what.
- Transfers, with care: a lore review that treats contradictions as bugs, because drift happened to Riot with many writers. We have one author and agents, so one owner file per society and a diff check on new cards suffices.
- Does not transfer: Riot's per-region naming conventions, because they are about human languages with localisation risk. Animal names are species names; our "convention" has to be about what a society calls its individuals, which Riot's sources do not cover.
- Does not transfer: a large shared map and many teams, because Riot's apparatus exists to keep dozens of writers aligned. At our scale a page per society is the whole machine; more would be the "do more" trap.
- Does not transfer: the claim that Riot built bibles first. The evidence says the reverse happened: scattered lore, a reboot that removed a frame, and consolidation years later. Our order (write the world first) is a different bet from theirs, not a copy.
- Does not transfer: fitting the world to the game, as Riot did with the Ionia civil war idea and with Bandle Tale, because we own both and have no player base attached to canon yet.

## Open questions the sources did not answer

- What does a region bible contain, how long is it, who is required to read it? No public source shows one.
- Are Riot's regional naming conventions written down, and who enforces them for LoR followers?
- How were hundreds of LoR followers named and given flavour from region material, and how often did the world not give enough?
- Did the LoR team find the lore too thin or too thick for card needs? Only indirect evidence (Ionia's civil war idea, Bandle City placement) exists.
- Is there a Riot account of a champion that failed because its region constrained the kit?
- Do the reboot's results (retention, player response to the story) support the claim that world-first pays off? Only Riot's reasoning is available, not outcomes.
- The Nexus articles (2017 naming, 2018 narrative, 2016 Universe) and the Building Runeterra dev diary video are the best primary sources and could not be read directly; reading them in full (or the video transcript) may change several points above.
