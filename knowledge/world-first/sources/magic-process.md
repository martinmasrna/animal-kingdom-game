# How Wizards of the Coast builds a Magic world and its cards

Research for the world-first question. Marking: "Source says" is what the page states; "Inference" is mine. Most pages were read through a summarising fetch, so quotes are short and should be re-checked against the page before being reused in a decision document. Several searched-for items turned out not to be public (a per-plane naming guide, a real world guide page); those are listed at the end rather than guessed at.

## 1. Sequence: when the world is built relative to the mechanics

**The stages.** Since Dominaria the pipeline is exploratory design, vision design, set design, play design. Before that there were two teams: "The Design team had the set for a year, and the Development team for about half a year", then "Instead of design and development, we have vision design, set design, and play design" ([Stages of Design](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/stages-of-design)). The month figures (two to three months exploratory, four vision) come only from a third-party summary and I did not confirm them in a Wizards page.

**A worldbuilder sits inside design from the start.** On Rosewater's podcast, the host (a vision design lead) says exploratory worldbuilding "starts roughly a month before exploratory design and continues through vision design", that a worldbuilding representative sits on the exploratory and vision teams, and that "vision design has to work very closely with world building" ([Drive to Work #1012, transcript](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/1012-working-with-worldbuilding), paraphrased by the fetch). The 2005 version says the same thing from the other side: Creative "most often begins creating cool new things during design once they see what the new mechanics are" ([Ninjas, Pirates, Myrs... Oh My](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/ninjas-pirates-myrs-oh-my-2005-01-31)). So in neither era is there a long period where the world exists alone and mechanics wait; the two overlap, and what differs is who holds the pen first.

**Top-down versus bottom-up.** In [Nuts & Bolts: Art](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/nuts-bolts-art-2019-02-11) Rosewater says "At Wizards, the art starts with the creation of the world", and that world building is either top-down (start from the flavor, Innistrad's gothic horror) or bottom-up (start from mechanics, Ravnica's guilds). In top-down, Design "tends to follow the lead of the Creative team more in the world-building space" while keeping mechanical space open; in bottom-up, Creative works out how to express the mechanics visually. [Nuts & Bolts: Creative Elements](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/nuts-bolts-10-creative-elements-2018-03-26) puts it as: top-down lets the setting drive the structure from the start, bottom-up settles mechanics first so flavor "has less obligation to guide early choices".

**Which order is better.** Rosewater does not claim either wins on quality. In the 2003 [Top-Down and Goal](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/top-down-and-goal-2003-06-09) he says "Top-down design is a lot harder than it looks", and that he does it more because players reacted strongly to it (Form of the Dragon). His reason is about the player: the aim is cards that "not only look flavorful but play flavorfully" ([C'mon, Innistrad Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/cmon-innistrad-part-1-2011-09-02)). He calls Innistrad "the first full top-down set where I led the design" and says the lesson was how potent it is to tap story elements players already know ([Lessons Learned Part 6](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/lessons-learned-part-6)). Inference: the stated benefit of world-first is not efficiency for the team but that the card plays like what it is.

**The mixed case is the norm.** Bloomburrow is the cleanest example. Vision design's handoff says "we went into the design knowing the resonant center, animals", yet the ten color-pair animal factions interlock mechanically, "a very bottom-up structure", with flavor layered on afterward ([Vision Design Handoff Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-1)). Ravnica was formally bottom-up (ten color pairs came first), yet the idea that turned pairs into guilds came from Creative (see section 3).

**What crosses the boundary.** The vision design handoff document. Innistrad's was organised into four "backbone" components (tribal monsters, graveyard, death matters, transformation) plus secondary themes per color and the ten two-color archetypes, and Rosewater says "it's key when making a handoff document" to convey priorities ([Innistrad Design Handoff Document](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/innistrad-design-handoff-document-2021-08-23)). Bloomburrow's was written by Beyer: predators, noncreature mechanics, food, color-pair archetypes, triad strategies. Its function is to hand over options, not final designs, so Set Design has "the freedom to use what works best"; many ideas (holidays, epic feats, the typal mechanic) were cut ([Handoff Part 3](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-3)). On the creative side the crossing document is the world guide (section 2). Inference: the handoff is a document of the world's shape as it matters to play, not the world itself.

## 2. The world guide and the concept push

**What it is.** In 2005: "a document known as a style guide that shows (and to a lesser extent tells) the artists what the new world is like", produced by the Creative Team before the art director can assign art for a new environment ([Ninjas, Pirates, Myrs](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/ninjas-pirates-myrs-oh-my-2005-01-31)). The podcast hosts add that it covers people, creatures, clothing, weapons and locales, runs to "hundreds of pages" (they describe it as an inch to an inch and a half thick in print), and that writers read it for stories and flavor text so everyone shares the same cosmology ([Drive to Work #478, Artists](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/478-artists); [#1012](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/1012-working-with-worldbuilding)). The weight is on pictures: reference images and concept paintings, with text secondary. Mood boards pull from real architecture and genre (gothic horror for Innistrad, Prague-like architecture for Ravnica). A "character grid" maps color against size and flying versus not so the creature list covers the range the game needs ([#1012](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/1012-working-with-worldbuilding); [Nuts & Bolts: Art](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/nuts-bolts-art-2019-02-11) mentions the same creature chart and says Creative normally makes it).

**The concept (world) push.** After vision design ends there is usually one push of about three weeks (the other episode says three to four) in which freelance artists are flown in and iterate with in-house concept artists, art directors and the worldbuilding team ([#1012](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/1012-working-with-worldbuilding); [#478](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/478-artists)). Ikoria shows it concretely: lead concept artist Sam Burley recruited five outside artists plus two in-house; the push began "after the first few worldbuilding phases were complete", ran three weeks, its reference images "filled the world guides", and illustrators then used those guides for the card art ([Worldbuilding Ikoria](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/feature/worldbuilding-ikoria-2020-04-07)). Andrew Vallas oversaw the world guide. Creative lead Doug Beyer calls his role "imagination-glue between a lot of smart folks". Mechanism, source plus inference: the push converts one set of ideas into a shared visual vocabulary quickly and cheaply before 100+ illustrators each invent their own version. Artists get "wide latitude early on, then gradually steer toward what works" (#478).

**Art descriptions.** Each card gets a concept first (what it represents, which leads to name, flavor text, "a direction for what kind of image") and then a short art description ([Nuts & Bolts: Art](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/nuts-bolts-art-2019-02-11)). The fields, in Wizards' own examples: Color (with card type), Location, Action, Focus, Mood ([You Make the Card 4](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/you-make-card-4-art-descriptions-2013-06-17)); the 2018 Ultimate Masters examples add Setting and Intent ([Ultimate Masters Art Descriptions](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/ultimate-masters-art-descriptions-2018-12-04)). Action is the longest field, a few sentences; Color, Focus and Mood are phrases. Real example (Life from the Loam, Action): "Show brash new plant life that has burst forth from the husks of abandoned undercity buildings." A story team member usually writes it, the art director tweaks it and assigns the artist; "we don't give composition to the artist most of the time" (#478). The box is small, so one clear element wins. The world guide constrains it by being the thing the artist reads alongside it; a Creative Bloq piece quotes an illustrator saying the job is to expand the prompt without contradicting the description ([Creative Bloq via search](https://www.creativebloq.com/features/10-stunning-card-art-illustrations), secondary). No full public world guide page was found.

## 3. Societies: how the units of a world are defined

Magic does not use one template. The units differ by set, and the pattern is that the unit is chosen to carry a structure the game already needs.

**Ravnica guilds (mechanics-first, flavor-made).** Per [City Planning Part II](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/city-planning-part-ii-2005-09-12), the design team was asked to revisit multicolor; Brady Dommermuth of Creative suggested making the ten color pairs distinct through flavored guilds; design liked it but "had no way to use it" and had about four months left. So the guild idea came from Creative, after a mechanical goal existed, late. Rosewater's basis: "Just as each color has an identity, each color pair has an identity" ([Magic Untapped interview](https://magicuntapped.com/index.php/features/mark-rosewater-talks-ravnica-guild-philosophies)). Design first tried every card in a guild, found ten keywords, ten cycles and ten victory routes "just too much", and split the guilds 4-3-3 across the block with about twenty placement criteria, including a list of guilds Creative thought "slam dunks creatively". Development said "You're asking us to jump off a cliff" (City Planning). The article does not publish the per-guild write-up; the public one-word descriptors (Selesnya nurturing, Golgari ambitious, Dimir manipulative, Boros proactive) come from a 2006 Dommermuth column, which I only saw second-hand via a forum quote. In the 2018 return, each guild got one keyword, and the stated job was "a guild's mechanical feel and finding a keyword that reinforced it", with each keyword required to work with the guilds sharing its colors ([Guild Order Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/guild-order-part-1-2018-09-10)). So here the mechanic is derived from the faction's feel, after the faction exists. Inference: the guild spec that mattered to cards was short: a color pair, a one-word temperament, a mechanical verb (Izzet: "about instants and sorceries").

**Bloomburrow species.** The faction is a species chosen for color history ("green-blue was really the only choice" for Frogs), with a size and personality each: Birds helpful and chipper, Rats cunning, Raccoons "resourceful, dumpster-diving rascals", Mice "small, brave heroes" ([Bloom of the Burrow Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/the-bloom-of-the-burrow-part-1); [Handoff Part 3](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-3)). Set Design "built each archetype to match the feel of the animal, then added a light sprinkling of typal cards". The frame is humans-as-animals: "a mouse is a normal person, so a bear, comparatively, is a terrifying monster"; predators are non-sentient "Calamity Beasts". Worldbuilding lead Neale LaPlante Johnson notes the ten factions are not "diametrically opposed" and that tension comes from the predators ([Dexerto interview](https://www.dexerto.com/magic-the-gathering/mtg-bloomburrow-worldbuilding-lead-once-worried-the-smash-hit-adorable-set-was-too-cute-2857856/)). This is the nearest analogue to Animal Kingdom, and worth noting: the societies are defined by temperament, scale and role, not by habitat.

**Lorwyn tribes.** Creative defined the flavor ("aggressive militaristic nature" for kithkin), Design built attacking mechanics, Development tuned power ([Kith and Kin](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/kith-and-kin-2007-10-22)). Rosewater's cost note: "Tribal as a theme is, by definition, very linear".

**Innistrad.** Units were horror tropes (monsters, victims, hunters); Jenna Helland's world distinctions (gothic zombies as flesh golems, which put blue into zombies) fed mechanics and color. Rosewater's rule against black eating everything: "if something could be done in a color other than black, we would do it in that color" ([C'mon, Innistrad](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/cmon-innistrad-part-1-2011-09-02)). Both directions occurred: mechanics needed red werewolves and creative made it fit; and the Eldraine and Kamigawa conflicts (courts versus wilds; tradition versus modernity, mapped to artifacts versus enchantments) were picked because they map onto structures design already had ([#1012](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/1012-working-with-worldbuilding)). The host adds "creative is a little more flexible than mechanics", so mechanics are usually the stricter constraint.

I did not find a public template listing what a faction write-up contains (how members are named, how they speak). Inference: the visible contents are color/temperament, look, role in the central conflict, and a mechanical verb.

## 4. Names

**Design names versus final names.** Designers name cards during design with informal "design names" and "the real names take a while"; Creative gives official names later ([By Any Other Name](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/any-other-name-2005-04-25)). The design-name techniques in that column are time-boxed (about 15 seconds for a real name, 30 for a funny one, then functional, allusion, alliteration, "Legion" names, running jokes, nonsense) and Rosewater generated roughly 50 candidates for one sample card. Those names are for discussion and signalling; for top-down sets, [Nuts & Bolts: Creative Elements](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/nuts-bolts-10-creative-elements-2018-03-26) says to label cards "after the trope you're trying to hit" and switch to realistic names before outside playtests.

**Who writes final names.** A named creative-team role: Brandon Bozzi, then Matt Cavotta, handled names and flavor text, after Brady Dommermuth's card concepting told them what each card is ([Ninjas, Pirates, Myrs](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/ninjas-pirates-myrs-oh-my-2005-01-31)). Order, per Nuts & Bolts: Art: concept first, then name, flavor text, image direction. Inference: a name is a downstream product of a concept, not an input to it, which is the mechanism behind the Animal Kingdom observation that Ash's legend produced his name.

**Rules.** From Creative Elements: avoid real card names (readers stop reading and compare); avoid words tied to other colors; suggest the function; creatures sound like creatures, instants take action verbs, lands are places, planeswalkers get character names; test pronunciation on a stranger; keep uncommon vocabulary for high rarities. Cavotta's "Okra, Twinkie, Tofu" sorted names into real-but-obscure words, invented words, and real roots combined, per [mtg.wiki and Star City Games summaries](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/all-flavor-is-made-for-someone/), secondary. A fan retelling says Ravnica's naming took about six months ([Say My Name](https://mtglore.com/web-articles/say-my-name/)), which is fiction-framed and should not be cited as fact.

**What I could not find:** a per-plane naming guide (Greek-ish for Theros, and so on) or a count of names generated per final card. The reference-pool idea is plausible from the mood-board practice but no source states it. Failure stories about names are mostly absent; the one documented tension is vocabulary: Werewolf was debated as a type name and Dommermuth argued for "Human Wolf" ([Innistrad Handoff](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/innistrad-design-handoff-document-2021-08-23)); Kaladesh was later renamed Avishkar, reason not established here.

## 5. Keeping it small

**How much reaches the card.** The world reaches cards through names, creature types, flavor text and art, and flavor text is optional: "It's not mandatory" and "More is less"; the best flavor text works in isolation ([Creative Elements](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/nuts-bolts-10-creative-elements-2018-03-26)). Art direction says "Figure out what matters to the card's flavor, and focus on that" ([Nuts & Bolts: Art](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/making-magic/nuts-bolts-art-2019-02-11)). Bloomburrow went further: noncreature cards carry the plane's flavor so creatures do not have to ("Noncreatures express the flavor of the plane, not its creatures"), and cameo animals were limited to uncommon and up because "We didn't want a Skunk archetype" ([Handoff Part 3](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-3); [Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-1)). Inference: the guide is large, the card is a thin slice, and the selection rule is "what a player can read in one glance".

**Resonance as the limit on absorption.** Rosewater's resonance lesson is that a player brings knowledge in: Innistrad leaned on horror tropes players already feel; Amonkhet's Egyptian mythology resonated less than staff expected because the audience knew less; Strixhaven's school experience varied by person ([Lessons Learned Part 6](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/lessons-learned-part-6)). Eldraine: fairy tales alone did not sell until paired with Arthurian lore. Inference: the player can absorb only the world they already half-hold; invented worlds must borrow a known frame (Bloomburrow borrowed "animals are people").

**Failures.** Original Kamigawa was "a top-down, flavor-centric block", "one of the worst-selling large sets of all time", with "the lowest ratings of any market research we'd done at the time" ([Stages of Design](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/stages-of-design)). That page does not blame top-down for it, and Rosewater's later diary criticises the block mechanically ("a disappointment mechanically"; wisdom "didn't really tie into the Japanese flavor") rather than its world ([Making of a Dynasty Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/making-dynasty-part-2-2022-01-31)). A summary from Inverse quotes him as saying it was experimental and "we made a lot of mistakes" and the first top-down block ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/champions-of-kamigawa-20-year-anniversary-magic-the-gathering), secondary). The honest reading is that the sources show a world-led block failing without isolating why; they do not show that overbuilding the world was the cause. Lorwyn is the cautionary case on structure: tribal flavor produced linear decks and players complained design was "building our decks for us" ([Kith and Kin](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/kith-and-kin-2007-10-22), and a [secondary summary](https://mtgrocks.com/mtg-lead-designer-hints-at-return-to-lorwyn/)). Ravnica's attempt to put all ten guilds in one set was "just too much", a cap on how many societies a player can hold at once. Bloomburrow's creative team worried the art was "too cute" and pushed pieces toward a more serious action feel; a rule against animal violence on cards also applied ([Dexerto](https://www.dexerto.com/magic-the-gathering/mtg-bloomburrow-worldbuilding-lead-once-worried-the-smash-hit-adorable-set-was-too-cute-2857856/)).

## 6. Small-team transfer

Source-grounded observations are above; the judgments are in the next section. The central structural fact from sources: Magic splits the work into roles (worldbuilding lead, story lead, art director, card concepter, names/flavor writer, vision designer) that meet at a few named artifacts (handoff document, world guide, concept, art description, name).

## What transfers to Animal Kingdom and what does not

- **Transfers: a world before the card, but only the slice a card can show.** Sources show names, types, flavor text and art as the only channels from world to card, and "More is less". A one-page-per-society bible fits that; a long guide would mostly go unread by one person.
- **Transfers: concept before name.** Magic orders it concept, name, flavor text, image direction. A legend written first producing the name and art (Ash) is the same mechanism, not luck. Failing cards (herds, City rats, Leopard) lack the concept step.
- **Transfers: define a society by temperament, scale and role, not habitat.** Bloomburrow's factions are one-line personalities plus size and a mechanical verb, and Ravnica's by color-pair temperament. Habitat-only groups give naming nothing to read.
- **Transfers: borrow a frame the reader already half-holds.** Resonance says invented worlds fail unless anchored. Animal Kingdom's anchor, real animals, is already stronger than most; the societies should lean on known animal reputations rather than invent lore.
- **Transfers: stage the societies.** Ravnica's all-ten-at-once playtest was "just too much". Fifty-two legendaries across eight families suggests writing a few societies fully first, as Ravnica did with 4-3-3.
- **Transfers with care: the art description fields (Location, Action, Focus, Mood).** The format is short and cheap and leaves composition to the painter, which suits a painted-by-model pipeline. Inference: an Intent line sourced from the society page does the world guide's constraining job.
- **Does not transfer: the concept push.** Its value is making many independent artists converge on one look. One painter pipeline already has one hand; the equivalent is a small set of reference paintings per society, not a three-week push.
- **Does not transfer: mood boards of real human architecture.** The Magic guide leans on clothes, buildings, weapons. Animal Kingdom has none of those; the bible's look sections would be fur, terrain, weather and behaviour.
- **Does not transfer: a separate naming role with pronunciation tests on strangers and a six-month schedule.** The rules (suggest function, sound distinct, pronounceable) transfer as a checklist; the staffing does not.
- **Caution, from Kamigawa and Lorwyn: a good world does not rescue the mechanics.** Sources show world-led failure modes (linear decks, mechanics that do not tie into flavor). Effects should not be forced out of society pages; the sources have mechanics derived from the faction's feel only as a verb ("about instants and sorceries"), and by Creative bending flavor to a structure.

## Open questions the sources did not answer

- What a real world guide page or a real faction write-up looks like; none is public, and the "hundreds of pages" claim is hearsay from a podcast.
- Whether a per-plane naming guide exists, what it specifies, and how many name candidates are produced per final card.
- Why original Kamigawa failed in world terms; every primary source blames mechanics or is silent.
- How Ravnica's guild write-ups were specified before cards; the primary articles give the Dommermuth origin and the 4-3-3 split but not the per-guild spec. The Dommermuth one-word list needs checking at the original Ask Wizards column.
- The durations of exploratory and vision design (I have only a third-party figure) and what the explicit handoff from worldbuilding to art contains beyond the guide.
- Whether any of this ordering produces better sets in measured terms; Rosewater's evidence is player reaction and market research, not an experiment.
