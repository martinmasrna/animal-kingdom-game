# The craft of the world bible as a working document

Research for the world-first question, 2026-10-24. Every claim carries its source. "Source says" marks what a source states; "Inference" marks my reading. Several things asked for were not found in public primary form; those gaps are named in the last section instead of papered over. The search tooling returned mostly secondary pages, so the evidence is thinner on documents' internal anatomy than on authors' stated principles.

## 1. Contents and length: what working bibles contain

What the public record shows is that the documents that actually circulated were short and did one of three jobs: sell the show, fix the tone for new hands, or give a department a lookup.

**The pitch bible is short and mostly characters and arc.** Source says: the Duffer Brothers' Stranger Things pitch ("Montauk") was a [23-page document](https://spyscape.com/article/stranger-things-secrets-from-the-duffer-brothers-pitch-bible-used-to-sell-the-show-to-hollywood) with an overview ("a love letter to the golden age of Steven Spielberg and Stephen King"), a plan for eight episodes, outlines for eleven characters each with backstory and planned arc, a three-act structure, a music and tone note, and a paragraph on how the supernatural is grounded and kept hidden. The article does not give page counts per section. Inference: the world is not a section here. Place and period live in the overview and tone note; most pages are people. The document exists to make a stranger believe the show, so it holds only what persuades.

**The tone manifesto does the work of a bible with no world in it.** Source says: Ronald D. Moore's Battlestar Galactica document, "Naturalistic Science Fiction, or Taking the Opera out of Space Opera", [opened with](https://www.mentalfloss.com/entertainment/tv/battlestar-galactica-facts) "Our goal is nothing less than the reinvention of the science fiction television series". It was suggested by producer David Eick to pitch executives and was then attached to scripts sent to actors; it reportedly helped draw Edward James Olmos. A scan of a 53-page "BSG Bible" dated 12/17/03 [is hosted online](https://www.generalstaff.org/FIC/Bibles/BSG_Bible.pdf); it is image-only and I could not read it, so I cannot say how the 53 pages split between essay, characters and world. Inference: the stated rule ("introduce realism into what has heretofore been an aggressively unrealistic genre", [quoted here](https://www.nerdunion.us/?p=14068)) is a constraint on how everything is rendered, not a catalogue of facts. It lets a new writer, actor or camera operator decide a thousand small things the same way. That is what a bible is for in practice: a generator, not an inventory.

**The writers' guide mixes a lookup with house rules.** Source says, via an auction description (secondhand, treat with caution): the Star Trek: TNG [Writers' Technical Manual](https://www.rrauction.com/auctions/lot-detail/li/349218707010915/cat/0) (third season, written by art-department staff Rick Sternbach and Mike Okuda) ran 37 pages in four sections: a technical primer, technical memoranda, emergency procedures, and a bestiary of celestial phenomena. The earlier first-season Writers/Directors Guide covered the script, the ship, and notes. [USC holds copies](https://archives.usc.edu/repositories/5/top_containers/17634?&page=2) in Ron Moore's files across seasons, so these were revised per season. Inference: the technical manual was written by the people who had to draw the ship, for the people who had to write it. The user and the author were the same crew, so it contains what writers kept getting wrong or asking about. That is the opposite of an author-driven table of contents.

**Who actually reads what.** I found no public account of which sections users open. Inference from the three cases above: characters and tone get read; geography, history and cosmology appear only as far as a scene needs them. The Blind Squirrel studio's [narrative design series](https://blindsquirrelentertainment.com/news/Narrative-Design-Part-2/) distinguishes an "IP Lore Bible" that onboards developers to existing source material from a game's own narrative bible about "OUR game concept, story, and characters", and puts both in pre-production. That is a studio's own taxonomy, not evidence of reading habits.

**What the tone rule costs when it hardens.** Source says: Roddenberry's informal "no petty conflict among the crew" rule (the [Roddenberry Box](https://www.cbr.com/star-trek-next-generation-roddenberry-box-explained/)) is credited by one writer (Brannon Braga) as a challenge that pushed conflict outside the ship, while former head writer Maurice Hurley called the vision "wacky-doodle"; the article notes it limited interpersonal drama. Another source says the rule is [not explicitly written](https://screenrant.com/star-trek-gene-roddenberry-vision-changes-explained/) in the TNG writers' guide, so it was an oral rule that behaved like a bible clause. Inference: a bible constraint is productive when it removes the default and forces invention (conflict must come from somewhere new), and expensive when it removes the thing the story needs.

## 2. The unit: society, place, character

I found no document that compares the organising units head to head, so what follows is mechanism by inference from working cases.

**Organising by faction gives every artefact a voice to match.** Source says: [Android: Netrunner](https://www.fantasyflightgames.com/en/news/2017/11/17/rule-the-world/) requires every deck to carry an identity card from one of its factions, and a design-discussion forum thread (community opinion, not an official document) says the [Corp factions were split](https://ffg-forum-archive.entropicdreams.com/topic/71258-why-4-corp-factions-and-3-runner-factions) around the four main effects a Corp can inflict. Magic's Ravnica is a [ten-guild plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravnica_(Magic:_The_Gathering)) built on the ten two-colour pairs. Inference: in both, the society is bolted to a mechanical identity, so a card's name, art and effect all have one place to read from. A faction is an answer to "who would own this card?", which a map cannot answer. The cost: a society can crowd out outsiders and creatures with no home; geography lets anything be a neighbour.

**Place organises the story, society organises the voice.** Source says: Riot's realms are published as a [Realms of Runeterra](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/Universe:Realms_of_Runeterra) book with distinct editorial credits (story editorial, background editorial, creative head). Warriors organises by [Clan, each "adapted to their own terrain"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series)), with naming that carries rank: a prefix from colour or environment, then "kit", "paw", a warrior suffix, or "star" for leaders. Inference: Warriors is the nearest cousin to our case (animals, communities, naming). Its names are produced by a rule that is the society's own (what a cat is called says where it stands), so a new name needs no committee.

**The one-page society sheet.** I did not find a public example I could read in full (the Ravnica guildmaster guide texts returned were unofficial mirrors, and the Avatar bible pages are not published online in anything I could open; [secondhand accounts](https://www.denofgeek.com/tv/avatar-the-last-airbender-two-college-friends-created-best-show-of-all-time/) only describe the pitch). The fields in the brief (values, look, speech, names, rituals, wants, fears, enemy, one scene) are therefore a design from first principles, not a copy of a real sheet. What the evidence supports is only this: Warriors shows naming and territory as the fields that did the most work, Netrunner shows a rule or constraint as a field, and Moore's document shows that one stated rule of rendering can replace pages.

## 3. Minimum viable: the iceberg and its limits

**The iceberg is about omission, and the omission has to be backed.** Source says: Hemingway's passage in *Death in the Afternoon* (1932) says a writer may omit what he knows and the reader [will have "a feeling of those things"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceberg_theory) if the writing is true enough, and ends with the warning that omission born of ignorance only makes "hollow places in the writing". Inference: the working claim is two-sided. Unseen depth shows through, and unseen emptiness also shows through. A bible earns its keep by what it prevents hollow, not by what it adds to the page.

**Sanderson shifts the claim from depth to its appearance.** Source says: in his lectures he says to ["give the illusion that there is an iceberg there. You don't actually have the iceberg"](https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/world-building-tips), the amount actually built being the author's choice "as long as the reader is convinced it is there". His 2025 lecture notes [say](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025): "Expand what you have before you add something new", "It's not about density of ideas. It's about how the ideas influence each other", and that writers should focus on what "directly impact[s] the characters and plot". A summary of his worldbuilding lectures [says](https://www.legendkeeper.com/brandon-sandersons-worldbuilding/) he "strongly encourages focusing on one physical element and no more than two cultural elements" and recommends "building just as much world as we need for the story"; the pyramid of abstraction puts concrete, simple language at the base. I could not retrieve a transcript, so these are second-hand renderings of the lectures. Note the tension with Hemingway: Sanderson permits faking the iceberg; Hemingway says a fake shows. Inference: faking is safe only while the story never tests the part you faked, which is cheap to guarantee for a book and expensive for a game with hundreds of cards that each test a corner.

**Tolkien built the language and let the world serve it.** Source says: in Letter 165 he writes ["The 'stories' were made rather to provide a world for the languages than the reverse"](https://glaemscrafu.jrrvf.com/english/introduction.html) and that his work is "fundamentally linguistic in inspiration". Inference: the generator was one deep thing (a language) and everything else was constrained by it. This is the strongest argument for a small bible with a real centre: pick the one generator, here arguably names or society voice, and let the rest be derived.

**Where it is contested.** Source says: M. John Harrison's 2007 post called worldbuilding ["the great clomping foot of nerdism"](https://vector-bsfa.com/2007/01/28/thought-for-the-day/), "the attempt to exhaustively survey a place that isn't there", and said every moment of a story "must represent the triumph of writing over worldbuilding", because it "numbs the reader's ability to fulfil their part of the bargain" ([full passage quoted in Marshall 2020](https://textjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/18569.pdf)). Marshall notes that Harrison was himself a skilled worldbuilder (Viriconium) and that the reply was a "torrent of invective", including the point that readers without literature degrees "tend to hate this stuff". Charlie Stross disagrees: worldbuilding is "like underwear: it needs to be there, but it shouldn't be on display", and gives characters "behavioural constraints". Marshall's survey also records Kate Elliott's argument that a minimalist approach can erode "the details which make specific cultures and their workings distinctive". Inference: these are not opposed on the main point. Harrison attacks display and the belief that the world does the reader's work; Stross and Sanderson want it hidden. The real disagreement is Elliott's: thin worlds default to the category mean, which here is the reader's stereotype. For an animal world that default (lion is regal, rat is dirty) is exactly the risk.

## 4. Keeping it alive

I found little public documentation of the working machinery; what exists is mostly about the failures.

**Consistency is held by roles, and the roles themselves split.** Source says: Warriors was [created and outlined by Victoria Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series)) (six-book arcs planned up front, with Kate Cary and Cherith Baldry writing first drafts); after her 2017 retirement the team grew. A [Publishers Weekly piece](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/childens/childrens-book-news/article/54425-harpercollins-introduces-a-new-erin-hunter.html) quotes the executive editor saying the Working Partners team sends "storylines and drafts of the manuscripts for every book" and that the process is "very much a group discussion". Critics note the large cast with confusing similar names. Riot's credits split [story editorial, background editorial, and creative head](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/Universe:Realms_of_Runeterra). Inference: with many hands, the bible is a person plus a review gate, not a file. With one author and agents, the equivalent is a single agent role that reads every artefact against the page.

**Canon fossilises, then breaks.** Source says: Riot rebuilt its lore in 2014, [dropping the Summoner concept](https://wiki.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/Universe:Canonical_inconsistencies) and much of the older material, and again in 2023 when unifying the Arcane and Runeterra universes overwrote much of the lore on Piltover, Zaun and yordles; fan wiki pages list inconsistencies Riot never resolved (secondary sources; I did not reach Riot's own posts). Inference: a world written for the cards that existed first cannot absorb the cards that come later without retcons. The cost of a late bible is that early artefacts contradict it. The cost of an early bible is that it is wrong by the third expansion.

**Lore outside the work does not count.** Source says: Destiny's first game put much backstory in [Grimoire cards that could not be read in the game](https://windowscentral.com/no-grimoire-destiny-2-pros-and-cons); Bungie dropped them in Destiny 2, with a developer saying he wants to "put the lore in the game" ([GameSpot](https://gamespot.com/articles/destiny-2-drops-grimoire-cards-to-showcase-the-sto/1100-6450228/) reports the cut). Insider claims of last-minute assembly were anonymous and are unverified. Inference: a bible that only the author reads has the same failure at small scale; if the artefact does not carry the world visibly or audibly, the world is not there for anyone.

## 5. From bible to artefact

**Hearthstone is the case the brief wanted, and the public record is thin on its mechanism.** Source says: Hearthstone was [set in Warcraft from early development](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Bad_cards), "featuring characters, powers and lore from that universe", with a team of WoW veterans. Lead designer Eric Dodds described the tone as ["2 parts epic and maybe 1 part ... whimsical fun"](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Bad_cards) and added "a few more dashes of charm and whimsy". On class heroes, Ben Brode said "In general it's hard to find a perfect fit". Minions were designed around "its numeric values, its title and its art" and were meant to feel like they ["belonged in the world of Azeroth"](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Design_and_development_of_Hearthstone). The Goblins vs. Gnomes set [began in a brainstorm](https://www.pcgamer.com/explosive-hearthstone-goblins-vs-gnomes-interview/) ("we love goblins and gnomes"), according to search summaries of the interview (I could not open the interview itself). I did not find Brode or Dodds using the word "resonance" for lore, nor a documented handoff from lore to card name. Inference: the Hearthstone team already shared one world in their heads and in a decade of game design, so no handoff document was needed. That is a luxury a one-person studio does have in a sense (one head), but agents do not share it; the document stands in for the shared memory.

**What a handoff might contain.** Nobody in the sources publishes a handoff template, and a Magic source says only that the creative team handles ["names, art, flavor text and world building"](https://www.tokensformtg.com/blog/detail/interview-with-mark-rosewater) after design, with no word on the brief's form. The Magic example is useful for one thing: it shows creative following design, not preceding it. Rosewater also said the [creative team grew](https://www.wargamer.com/magic-the-gathering/mark-rosewater-creative-team) from about five people (Shards of Alara) to "many times larger" and attributes differences in worldbuilding depth to available hours. Inference: depth tracks labour, so with agents the temptation is depth because it is cheap.

**Where character actually comes from in the cases that worked.** Source says: Supergiant's Kasavin said Hades' [approach hinged](https://butwhytho.net/2021/08/13/interview-developing-hades-with-supergiant-games-greg-kasavin/) on seeing the Olympians as "one big dysfunctional family", which let the team expand a character little known from myth, with a [22,000-line script by under 20 people](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1026975/contactUs) (GDC listing). Hollow Knight's Ari Gibson says a world "just needs points of interest and connections" ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/when-we-made-hollow-knight/)). Inference: in both, character came from a relationship structure (family, connection), not from a geography or a history. That matches the society-first hypothesis, with the point that the one useful fact about a society is who stands in what relation to whom.

## 6. Failure

**Worldbuilder's disease is real as practitioner opinion, with no measurement.** Source says: advice writing defines it as [the world absorbing the story](https://www.tckpublishing.com/worldbuilders-disease/) and offers causes such as fear of losing ideas and love of the activity; a ["elven shoes" example](https://www.goodreads.com/author/show/66471.Chris_Baty/blog?page=39) (Chris Baty) names time spent on unneeded parts of a setting; critics reply that [much of the complaint targets franchise expansion](https://thecommabandit.bearblog.dev/criticising-worldbuilding/) rather than building worlds. I found no study of how much lore goes unused or how often projects stall, so treat the term as folk wisdom. Inference: the risk for agents is higher than for humans because the usual brake (the author's tired hands) is absent.

**Documented over-reach by authority.** The Roddenberry Box above is the clean example of a document constraining the work it was meant to help: the guide is not the problem, the unexamined prohibition is.

**Right size and right time, as authors put it.** Source says: Wendig builds the world [after character and plot, warning](https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/world-building-tips) that world-building that leads "can become unwieldy and hold the story back"; Schwab fills in details only "as they are relevant to context and character perspective"; Bardugo calls deciding how much to reveal "the hardest part". Sanderson's own advice, "expand what you have before you add something new". Inference: the consensus among practitioners is just-in-time, driven by the next thing that needs it. The dissent (Elliott, Tolkien) is that some one thing has to be deep and early.

## What transfers to Animal Kingdom and what does not

- **Transfers: the bible as a generator of rendering decisions, not an inventory.** Moore's one-page rule and Tolkien's language-first show that one deep generator replaces many facts. For us the generator is society (who names whom, what shape a society has), because that is what a name or art brief reads from. Reason: a card needs a decision, not a fact.
- **Transfers: one society at a time, driven by a card that forces it.** Wendig, Schwab and Sanderson's "expand what you have" agree on just-in-time. Reason: it is the only version of a bible that has a test (does the card now come easily?) built in.
- **Transfers: the fields that did real work in the evidence are naming rule, territory or home, and relation to others.** Warriors' suffix-by-rank names and Hades' family frame carried the most. Reason: they are the ones a name or art brief can use directly. I would not carry over values, rituals and "one scene" until a card asks for them; that part is my judgement, not a finding.
- **Transfers: the artefact must show the world, or the world is not there.** The Grimoire case. Reason: hidden lore is not worldbuilding; in our case a legendary's name and painting must carry the society without the page.
- **Transfers with care: Hemingway's two-sided iceberg.** The claim that unseen depth shows is supported, but his warning that omission from ignorance leaves hollow places is the one that matters; Sanderson's "illusion" version works only until a card tests the faked part. Reason: a card game tests every corner.
- **Transfers: Elliott's warning about thin worlds defaulting to stereotype.** For animals the stereotype (lion regal, rat filthy) is the category mean; a society page's main job may be to give the animal something other than its stereotype. Reason: Inference from Elliott's argument applied to our case.
- **Does not transfer: the multi-hand governance (editors, canon tiers, a keeper).** The Warriors and Riot machinery exists because many writers diverge. Reason: one author with agents needs a single reader of the page, not a hierarchy.
- **Does not transfer: Hearthstone's method.** Their team's shared head of Warcraft did the work, and the public record shows no document. Reason: we have no pre-existing world, so the bible is the first copy, not a record of one.
- **Does not transfer: pitch-bible conventions (arcs, seasons, three-act structure).** Reason: those serve selling a narrative sequence; a card game has no sequence.
- **Does not transfer: the full-world lookup (cosmology, history, geography).** TNG's technical manual earned its 37 pages because the ship had to be drawn. Reason: we have no equivalent object that must be drawn consistently except the animals themselves.

## Open questions the sources did not answer

- What a real one-page society or faction sheet looks like, field by field, and which fields its users open. No public example I could read; the Avatar and Ravnica bibles were not retrievable as primary texts.
- What the actual handoff document is between a bible and a card name or art brief at Hearthstone, Magic or Riot. None is published in anything I found.
- Whether Hearthstone's team used the word "resonance" or an equivalent, and how they chose which Warcraft characters made cards. The wiki says only that fits were hard and tone was adjusted.
- Whether the one deep generator is better chosen early or discovered from the first cards that work. Tolkien chose early; the card-first cases (Goblins vs. Gnomes) discovered it.
- How a bible stays honest when agents write it: whether a hollow, plausible-sounding page is detectable. No source addresses AI-authored bibles, and my "agents overbuild" point is an inference from Rosewater's labour remark.
- Whether a society page should be tested by something other than the next card (for example, asking a cold reader to name an animal from the page). No source gives a test.
- Primary text of the BSG bible (53 scanned pages), the Marshall article's full list of Harrison's critics, Sanderson's lecture transcript, and Riot's own canon posts were not read in full; claims about them are second-hand as marked.
