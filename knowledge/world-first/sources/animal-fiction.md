# How animal fiction built its societies, naming and limits

This file records what the published record says about how the best animal fiction made its societies, who names whom in them, and how the authors kept the world small. Where a source states something, the claim is linked. Where I draw a conclusion the source does not draw, it is marked "Inference".

Source quality varies. Interviews, the books' own text and publisher or author pages are primary. Wikipedia and fan essays are secondary, and are used mostly for naming systems. Several fan wikis (Redwall, Watership Down) refused automated access, so some Redwall and Watership Down detail comes from secondary summaries. No source found describes a written "series bible" for Warriors, and nobody has published the Disney or Adams world notes. The gaps are listed at the end.

## 1. The unit of society

**Warriors: a kin-and-territory group fused with a religion.** The Clans began as a structural idea before the setting existed. Victoria Holmes says the idea of "four groups living in different locations, close together" came first and the New Forest suited it afterwards: "she wasn't inspired by the forest itself" ([BuzzFeed interview](https://www.buzzfeed.com/andrewfirriolo/erin-hunter-warriors-books-interview), summarised). Wikipedia notes that "Clan" is capitalised because each is tied to its territory and its religion, and that each adapted to its terrain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series))). The unit buys conflict (borders follow rivers, food competition drives the plots) and a recognisable team identity: a reader can say "I'm ThunderClan". Inference: four peer groups of one species give the writer symmetry, so any story can be told as one Clan against another with the same vocabulary.

**Watership Down: a warren as a political form.** Each warren is a different way of organising rabbits. Sandleford is a complacent hierarchy whose Chief Rabbit dismisses a warning. Cowslip's trades freedom for comfort under a farmer who feeds and snares them. Efrafa is a police state with branded Marks, a Council and an Owsla that enforce obedience. Watership Down is a small group where the leader listens and members contribute different talents ([encyclopedia.com analysis](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/watership-down), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watership_Down)). The choice of unit is the book's engine: the quest is a walk through governments. Adams denied this was allegory, calling it "only a made-up story" and "in no sense an allegory or parable" ([Wikipedia summary of his statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watership_Down)). Inference: whether or not he meant it, readers learn a warren by what it does to a rabbit, so a society is defined by one dominant bargain (security for freedom, comfort for knowledge of snares).

**Redwall: an institution plus a moral type.** The Abbey is the home of the good woodlanders; the threats are vermin (rats, weasels, stoats, foxes, snakes and other predators), while mice, hares, badgers, otters, moles and squirrels are the good species ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redwall)). Roles repeat: Badger Lords, the Skipper of Otters, Log-a-Log the shrew leader. Species fixes the side, the institution fixes the stakes. The critique is real: Ruiz writes that "you can tell a black hat from a white hat just by species" and that "lineage dictates character" ([LARB](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/badgers-in-platemail-on-brian-jacquess-redwall-series/)). A reviewer summary reports that species is sometimes treated as a career ("searat") and sometimes as innate savagery ([TV Tropes discussion](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Headscratchers/Redwall), as summarised by search). Jacques bought instant legibility for children and paid with moral flatness.

**The Jungle Book: a pack and a Law.** The Seeonee pack meets at Council Rock; the people call themselves the "Free People"; membership has a procedure (a cub must be vouched for by two non-parents) ([Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/236/236-h/236-h.htm), via search summary). Other species (Bagheera, Kaa, Shere Khan) are outsiders with their own relation to the Law.

**Mouse Guard: a mixed institution over small cities.** The Guard is an institution created by war: "a recent war against weasels created a need for a strong cadre of guardmice"; the mouse territories are linked by dangerous land ([CBR interview](https://www.cbr.com/david-petersen-talks-mouse-guard/)). Petersen organised his building in three tracks, "Costumes & Characters, History & Culture, and Locations and Architecture" ([lecture note](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/18739177-worldbuilding-lecture-video)). Other species are not citizens but dangers; the predator-prey relations are real, "the animals are actually animals" (as reported in search from a Petersen interview).

**Zootopia: a place, built on one habitat rule.** Society is a city; districts are habitats (Tundratown, Sahara Square, Rainforest District, Little Rodentia). The rationale came from a writer's question: neighbourhoods have "gone out of their way to make the citizens comfortable" ([Awards Daily](https://www.awardsdaily.com/2016/12/27/interview-rich-moore-byron-howard-discuss-disneys-zooptopia-creating-world-animals-talk-walk-upright/)). Species is not a district: the story's tension is predator against prey inside one city, and the film's bias plot depends on species mixing. Howard says "all those districts we didn't have room for in the first film" ([Zootopia News Network](https://www.zootopianewsnetwork.com/2017/03/exclusive-interview-with-byron-howard.html)). Inference: places and species are two axes; Zootopia uses place for texture and species for conflict, a split that matches the Animal Kingdom hypothesis (societies over geography).

**Farthing Wood: a pact, not a place.** The animals leave their wood and swear an Oath to protect each other until White Deer Park ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Animals_of_Farthing_Wood)). Several species share one society by rule: predators set aside instinct. A reader critique says the logic strains because "Adder, Weasel, even Fox and Vixen" could not survive without eating mice ([TV Tropes](https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Literature/TheAnimalsOfFarthingWood), as summarised). When species share a society, the role is fixed by an explicit bargain or a job (Guard, Abbey); when the bargain is merely stated and then not paid for, readers notice.

**Others.** In Bambi the unit is the family and the forest hierarchy: princes live apart from does and young, and the Old Prince is a teacher ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambi,_a_Life_in_the_Woods)). Fantastic Mr Fox has an underground neighbourhood of Fox, Badger, Moles, Rabbits and Weasels who share starvation, then a shared hidden home ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fantastic_Mr_Fox)). Kung Fu Panda has a valley of mixed species with each master a species ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kung_Fu_Panda)). Beastars splits one school into carnivores and herbivores ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beastars)).

## 2. Who names whom

**Warriors: the best-copied system, and a rule set.** A kit is named by its parents with a nature word; at six moons the leader gives the "-paw" apprentice name; at warrior ceremony the leader or mentor adds a new suffix chosen for the cat's personality and the rhythm of the whole; leaders take "-star" ([Warriors official site](https://warriorcats.com/content/article/editors-and-choosing-warrior-names), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series))). Holmes calls each name "a teeny tiny poem." Rank is visible in the word, the prefix stays for life, and a change of status is a naming event (Flamekit refused Flameheart and became Nightheart, so even the namer's choice can be rejected).

The editors state constraints, and the official page is the closest thing to a rule list in public:
- names use natural elements "that commonly occur in the UK, since the cats are unofficially British";
- Squash Blossom became Apple Blossom because squash does not grow wild in the UK;
- rare animals (elk, moose, coyote) are mostly kept out;
- exceptions exist: kittypet-born cats keep names like Garfield or Macgyver, which counts as a sign of origin ([Warriors official site](https://warriorcats.com/content/article/editors-and-choosing-warrior-names)).

The team says all three authors contribute names and that names draw on "body parts, fur colors, and forest sights, sounds, and scents" ([Writer Unboxed, 2006](https://writerunboxed.com/2006/04/21/author-interview-erin-hunter/)).

**The Hammerclaw story is the key failure case.** Tigerclaw was Hammerclaw in the first manuscript. Days before printing someone asked "How would a cat know what a hammer is?" and it became Tigerclaw ([Warriors official site, "ten secrets"](https://warriorcats.com/content/article/erin-hunter-reveal-ten-secrets-from-the-warrior-cats-books)). The official names page adds the principle: a hammer is a human tool and "it seemed strange for cats to know" one. Mechanism: the reader tolerates a word the animals could not know only until someone asks the question; the question then breaks the point of view. Inference: this check was applied by a human editor late; a written rule would have caught it at the draft stage.

Other failures the team admits: Runningnose (a pun now felt to be unfortunate), Foxheart (became a common insult), Twigbranch (criticised by fans) ([official names page](https://warriorcats.com/content/article/editors-and-choosing-warrior-names)). Wikipedia lists a large cast with similar names among the criticisms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series))). Inference: a prefix-suffix grammar gives unlimited names but also many similar ones, so collision is the cost of a productive system. The official page also calls Echosong admired but too lyrical to be a typical warrior name, so register is policed as well as plausibility.

**Watership Down: two names per rabbit, one for the reader and one for the rabbits.** Rabbits have English names (Hazel, Fiver, Bigwig, Pipkin) and Lapine names that describe: Fiver is Hrairoo ("Little One of Many"), Bigwig is Thlayli ("Fur-head", after a tuft of fur), Pipkin is Hlao-roo ("Little Dimple in the Grass"); Hazel becomes Hazel-rah, "Chief Hazel", once he leads; the Threarah takes his title from "rowan tree", because one grows near Sandleford ([list of characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Watership_Down_characters)). So the Lapine name is a seen mark or an object near the warren, and status is a suffix. The English name is the translation the reader needs. Adams built the language as he went: "I just constructed Lapine as I went—when the rabbits needed a word for something so did I" ([Lapine language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapine_language)). He wanted it to sound "wuffy, fluffy" (same article). Numbers stop at four; above that is "hrair", so the grammar itself tells the reader how rabbits think. Kathryn Hume argues (as summarised, not quoted) that the vocabulary substitutes for genuine strangeness, so the rabbits end up as humans with fur ([encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/watership-down)).

**Redwall: names by species, with a human stock.** A fan essay argues the names borrow from British history and lore (the cellar keeper Ambrose Spike may echo Ambrosius Aurelianus) and Jacques drew characters from people he knew: Gonff from himself as a boy near the Liverpool docks, Mariel from his granddaughter, Constance the Badgermum from his grandmother ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Jacques)). Species gives a title (Badger Lord, Skipper) and a dialect; the individual name is a human-style name. Inference: it is easy for a child to remember because the name is half a person.

**The Jungle Book: names as nicknames from the body or a fault.** Akela is "the great gray Lone Wolf"; Shere Khan's mother "did not call him Lungri [the Lame One] for nothing"; Mowgli is named by Mother Wolf: "for Mowgli the Frog I will call thee" ([Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/236/236-h/236-h.htm), via search summary). The animals name a thing from its look or defect, and the name sticks as a taunt.

**Mouse Guard: a borrowed human stock chosen for sound.** Names have a Welsh or Gaelic feel and come partly from baby-name books and their etymologies, partly from how they sound ([CBR](https://www.cbr.com/david-petersen-talks-mouse-guard/)).

**Zootopia: species names plus puns.** Judy Hopps, Nick Wilde, Duke Weaselton, Flash the sloth at the "Department of Mammal Vehicles", Mr Big the shrew ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zootopia)). The pun is the naming rule; it works because the film is a comedy about human institutions.

**Bambi: titles over names.** Humans are "He", and the male deer are "princes" with the "Old Prince" as the elder ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambi,_a_Life_in_the_Woods)). The Old Prince is a title, never a personal name.

## 3. The bible

**Warriors.** The most useful finding is that no public "bible" document exists, and the system works through people. Holmes, the editor, sets each series' direction and protagonists in advance, plans six-book arcs, and writes the storyline. A new book's outline takes about two months and covers "every single scene" plus suggested dialogue, and runs to about half the length of the finished book (about 30,000 words per the BuzzFeed account; [January Magazine](https://januarymagazine.com/2008/06/author-snapshot-victoria-holmes.html), [BuzzFeed](https://www.buzzfeed.com/andrewfirriolo/erin-hunter-warriors-books-interview)). Writers receive "plotline, notes, cast lists." She keeps "very detailed lists of character names and descriptions, the rituals used by the Clan, and what happened in previous stories" ([Writer Unboxed](https://writerunboxed.com/2006/04/21/author-interview-erin-hunter/)). The draft comes back to her, and she spends "a week or so" checking that it sounds like "Erin" and that the story works as she imagined.

What this says about the mechanism: consistency is held by three artifacts (the outline, the cast-and-ritual lists, the editor's read) rather than a long essay on the world. Names are checked at the editing stage, which is where Hammerclaw was caught. Inference: the "bible" is a continuity ledger that grows as books are written, and for Series One it did not even contain family ties ("the author had no plan for how the cats were related in Series One; they only planned six books, so they created only the cats the stories needed", [ten secrets](https://warriorcats.com/content/article/erin-hunter-reveal-ten-secrets-from-the-warrior-cats-books)). The world was built to the size of six books and extended when the series went on.

Not found: any account of how a new ghostwriter is briefed beyond the outline and lists, any page count of a bible, and any list of forbidden words other than what the names page implies. Reviews of the cast size and the theocracy comparison are the only public comment on what the written world cost.

**Watership Down.** Adams improvised the story for his daughters on car journeys, then wrote it in the evenings; behaviour comes from Lockley's *The Private Life of the Rabbit* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watership_Down)). Lockley supplied the facts, and Adams discovered the book after drafting much of the story (search summary of a Wikipedia mirror). His chapter epigraphs come from Aeschylus, Bunyan, Malory, Gilgamesh, Campbell and de la Mare ([encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/watership-down)). The record shows no Adams world document; the fixed points were an ethology book and the oral story. Lapine was created in the writing, "as I went."

**Mouse Guard.** Petersen's own sources are rich. He built three tracks (costume and character, history and culture, locations and architecture), maps with Michigan places (Darkwater, Dawnrock), a timeline to Fall 1152 with a Black Axe prequel in 1099-1116, and used guide pages ([CBR](https://www.cbr.com/david-petersen-talks-mouse-guard/)). The RPG designer Luke Crane forced him to answer how a mouse becomes a Guard, how money works, city sizes and government: Crane wanted "to flesh out the world more and give him boundaries to work in" ([Surman interview](https://www.stevensurman.com/musings-mice-comic-book-creator-interview-david-petersen/)). Petersen estimates "80-90%" of the RPG is canon. Some cities are left for players. So the written world was mostly produced on demand by a second person's questions, not before the work.

**Zootopia.** Research took 18 months by the team's account: Disney's Animal Kingdom, Kenya, San Diego Zoo Safari Park, a microscope look at fur; experts on accessibility, HVAC, vehicles and urban planning were consulted so that a city could work for mammals from about 5 cm to 8 m tall ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zootopia), [Variety, via search excerpt](https://variety.com/2016/artisans/production/zootopia-posed-unique-challenges-for-disney-1201721138/)). Production designer David Goetz says everything was built to real scale and that of ten burrows built, five are in the film. Howard says there were districts "we didn't have room for." The one documented design rule is that the world's logic has to answer a question (how is a polar bear comfortable here?) rather than describe the world. Inference: a large amount of the built world is left off screen on purpose, and the districts that appear are chosen for story.

**Redwall.** Jacques wrote an 800-page handwritten manuscript from stories he told blind children and a style that foregrounded "sound, smell, taste, touch" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Jacques)). No bible is documented.

## 4. Learning a society from one scene

The devices that carry a society fastest, with the work that shows them:
- **A rule said aloud.** The Jungle Book's council opens with "Ye know the Law—ye know the Law. Look well, O Wolves!" and the Law is quoted when a cub needs two voices to vouch for it ([Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/236/236-h/236-h.htm), via search summary).
- **A ritual that changes a name.** In Warriors, a naming ceremony shows the Clan's authority, its respect for rank and its tone in one scene; the reader gets Clan structure from the new suffix.
- **A branded body.** Efrafa's Marks tell the reader a rabbit belongs to the state before anyone speaks.
- **What the society will not talk about.** Cowslip's rabbits refuse to discuss the snares ([character list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Watership_Down_characters)); the silence is the lesson.
- **A fear and a refusal.** Sandleford's Threarah dismisses a warning, which defines a complacent warren in one exchange.
- **A territory sketched by habitat.** Zootopia's districts are readable at a glance because each is a climate with a look, and the film's visual rule (a climate wall between Sahara Square and Tundratown) is a picture, not a lecture ([Variety, via search excerpt](https://variety.com/2016/artisans/production/zootopia-posed-unique-challenges-for-disney-1201721138/)).
- **Food and manners.** Redwall's food descriptions and Dibbuns (infant woodlanders) are the Abbey's tone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redwall)).
- **An origin myth.** StarClan: Holmes wanted the belief to "chime with their natural environment," so dead cats are eyes in the stars; the ancestor sky explains the Clan's authority ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series))).

Inference: each device is a single, specific, checkable fact about what the society does or refuses. None is an adjective about the society. A card painting plus a line could carry a rule, a mark or a refusal, not a history.

## 5. Keeping human things out

**Renaming the human thing in the animals' terms.** Fire in The Jungle Book: "By Red Flower Bagheera meant fire, only no creature in the jungle will call fire by its proper name" ([Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/236/236-h/236-h.htm), via search summary). Watership Down gives a car the sound-word "hrududu" (with the plural "hrududil"), partly onomatopoeic, as Adams said ([Lapine language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapine_language)). Warriors calls humans "Twolegs", roads "thunderpaths" and cars "monsters", though Holmes "dislikes the name for cars" and says she didn't come up with it ([ten secrets](https://warriorcats.com/content/article/erin-hunter-reveal-ten-secrets-from-the-warrior-cats-books)). Bambi's humans are "He" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambi,_a_Life_in_the_Woods)). In each case the animal word is made from what the animal perceives: a flower, a sound, a limb count, a pronoun. Inference: the rule works when the word names the animal's experience of the thing, not the thing's function.

**Closing the world entirely.** Later Redwall books "ignore humans completely," with an Iron Age society of castles, bridges and ships, though the first book mentions a horse-drawn cart ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Jacques)). Mouse Guard keeps the cities and swords but moves the human world out: medieval, "it's mice" ([CBR](https://www.cbr.com/david-petersen-talks-mouse-guard/)). So the breaking of the rule works when the human things are a coherent older layer (swords, abbeys) that implies no modern human, and the animals make them. The cost: critics read the Abbey as property order treated "as naturally righteous" ([LARB](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/badgers-in-platemail-on-brian-jacquess-redwall-series/)).

**Open mixing.** Zootopia and Kung Fu Panda give the animals clothes, phones and cities. Howard's pitch was animals who "walk upright, wear clothes and carry cell phones" ([Awards Daily](https://www.awardsdaily.com/2016/12/27/interview-rich-moore-byron-howard-discuss-disneys-zooptopia-creating-world-animals-talk-walk-upright/)). The cost is that the animals are people, and the interest shifts to human issues (bias, institutions). Variety reports that the architecture avoids "glass, concrete, and steel" in favour of natural materials ([Variety, via search excerpt](https://variety.com/2016/artisans/production/zootopia-posed-unique-challenges-for-disney-1201721138/)), a partial attempt to keep the animals' look inside a human conceit. Fantastic Mr Fox keeps animals animal and lets humans be the enemy with tractors and cider cellars ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fantastic_Mr_Fox)).

**A criticism of the closed approach.** Hume, as summarised there, says Adams's vocabulary substitutes for genuine strangeness ([encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/watership-down)). A world with a new vocabulary and human psychology underneath can still read as people in fur. Warriors has the same pattern: the series uses cats to explore human concepts like politics and territoriality (Wikipedia's wording, summarised) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series))). Closing the world in the vocabulary does not close it in the psychology.

## 6. Mythology and history

**Watership Down** interleaves the El-ahrairah tales with the story. Frith is the creator, El-ahrairah the trickster, the Black Rabbit of Inlé the death figure. Adams said the rabbits "believed passionately in El-ahrairah" rather than worshipped, and the tales are lighter interludes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watership_Down)). They "build a shared heritage and model virtues the rabbits value: cleverness, humor, self-sacrifice, and loyalty" and the travellers' own history becomes myth by the end ([encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/educational-magazines/watership-down)). The name El-ahrairah is built from the sound system: "Elil + hrair + rah", "Prince with a Thousand Enemies" ([Lapine language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapine_language), via search summary). The myth names the species' situation: many enemies, a trickster's answer.

**Warriors.** StarClan began as the name of the central Clan; when Holmes added a religious dimension, "the name fit the ancestors in the sky better," and the Clan became ThunderClan because it lay near the thunderpath ([ten secrets](https://warriorcats.com/content/article/erin-hunter-reveal-ten-secrets-from-the-warrior-cats-books)). The past in Warriors is mostly a living authority: ancestors guide by prophecy and give leaders nine lives. The religion carries a political cost; Wikipedia says its politics have been compared to a theocracy, and later arcs changed the code (mates from different Clans) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warriors_(novel_series))). A myth that grants rules also makes rewriting them a story event.

**Mouse Guard** puts its past into chapter-opening ballads and a dated timeline, and Petersen notes legends "are the legends and stories the mice tell, and we know those rarely are complete truths" ([Surman interview](https://www.stevensurman.com/musings-mice-comic-book-creator-interview-david-petersen/)). That gives a way to add history without contradicting canon: the animals tell it, and it may be wrong.

**The Jungle Book** has the Law, "which never orders anything without a reason," and Master Words ([Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/236/236-h/236-h.htm), via search summary). The past appears as rules with stated reasons.

**Redwall** invokes Martin the Warrior as a founder repeatedly ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redwall)): one named ancestor for the whole institution. **Farthing Wood** has the Oath as an event the group remembers.

Inference for naming: in Watership Down and Warriors the mythology also supplies the name vocabulary (hrair, star), so names are themselves compressed history.

## What transfers to Animal Kingdom and what does not

- **Transfers: make the names carry status, as Warriors does.** A suffix or title that changes at a rank event teaches a society's structure with every name. Reason: it lets one painting and one name show who is senior. Limit: Animal Kingdom's legendaries are single individuals, so the title has to carry what a ceremony would.
- **Transfers: a name is what a seen mark or deed gives, as in Watership Down and Jungle Book.** Thlayli ("Fur-head"), Lungri ("the Lame One"), Mowgli ("the Frog") are all things an animal can see. This matches the existing rule that every word comes from the animal's world.
- **Transfers: one dominant bargain per society.** Efrafa (obedience for safety) and Cowslip's (comfort for ignorance) are learned in a scene because each is one sentence long. Reason: a page per society should lead with that bargain, since a card line can carry one.
- **Transfers: write what the animals cannot know as a list, before naming.** Hammerclaw was caught only days before print; the official page also names UK-only plants and absent species. Reason: a short forbidden list (tools, materials, plants and animals outside the society's range) catches a bad name at draft time.
- **Transfers: rename the human thing from the animal's perception.** Red Flower, hrududu, Twolegs. Animal Kingdom has no humans, so this applies only where a card must refer to a human thing; it supports the rule against tools and materials.
- **Transfers in part: keep the bible as a ledger.** Holmes's tool was outlines plus lists of names, rituals and past events, grown book by book. Reason: a short page per society that lists names taken and events that happened serves the same function. Does not transfer: her two-month, half-book outlines, which exist to brief ghostwriters.
- **Does not transfer: species as moral type.** Redwall's critique shows what a legible species-to-side map costs. Animal Kingdom's families are habitats and decks, and legendaries are individuals, so a society should be defined by a bargain or a shape, not by "the lions are noble".
- **Does not transfer: Zootopia's open mixing.** Clothes, phones and puns make the animals people, which contradicts the closed world. The thing worth taking is its logic of districts that answer a question, and its habit of building more than is shown.
- **Does not transfer: Warriors' ancestor religion as the main myth.** It generates plots (prophecy, nine lives) but needs rewriting when rules change. For a card game, a society's past should be one or two events, not a faith.
- **Does not transfer easily: a full invented language.** Lapine took a novel to establish and critics say it can substitute for strangeness. Use a small word stock, a few sound-words, not a grammar.

## Open questions the sources did not answer

- Whether the Warriors editors ever had a written style or naming guide for new writers; every public account describes outlines, lists and an editor's read, none a document or its length.
- How Gillian Philip, Inbali Iserles or Tui Sutherland were actually briefed.
- Adams's own account of why the principal rabbits have English names, and whether he drew up a map of the four warrens before writing; the sources here give only the improvised-oral origin and his "as I went" remark.
- How Jacques chose names and whether he kept any notes; no primary source was found.
- What Disney's story room actually wrote down about world rules (the "no tools" idea, scale rules, species rules); the sources describe research and constraints, not a document.
- Whether Petersen's "guide pages" are what readers see in the books or a separate working set.
- How readers measure the cost of each device (does a naming ceremony or a refusal really teach faster?); all claims in section 4 are inference from the works, not from reader studies.
- What happens to a society's tone when many writers extend it; Warriors is the only case and its reception is described in terms of cast size and prose, not tonal drift.
- Primary-source access: Fandom pages for Redwall and Watership Down (including the Adams interview "I just can't do humans") were blocked, and several news pages (Variety, Deadline) only gave search excerpts, so quotes from those are second-hand.
