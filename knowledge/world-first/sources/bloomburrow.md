# Bloomburrow: how a world of animal societies was built and what the cards, names and art took from it

Method note. The pages below were read through a fetch tool that summarises with a small model, so quoted phrases are as that tool returned them from the Wizards pages; a quote that carries weight for a decision should be re-checked on the page. Where a line is my reading rather than the source's, it is marked "Inference".

## 1. The unit of society: what one culture entry contains

The unit is a species, and the species is pinned to a colour pair. Ten "animalfolk" (Birds, Rats, Lizards, Raccoons, Rabbits, Bats, Otters, Squirrels, Mice, Frogs) map to the ten two-colour pairs ([Vision Design Handoff, Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-1)). Cameo animals (Skunk, Hedgehog, Badger, Weasel, Beaver and others) exist but never become an archetype.

The culture write-ups sit in the public [Planeswalker's Guide, Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/planeswalkers-guide-to-bloomburrow-part-2). My read of it: only five species get a full entry (Birdfolk, Ratfolk, Lizardfolk, Raccoonfolk, Rabbitfolk), each roughly 650 to 900 words. The other five (Bats, Otters, Squirrels, Mice, Frogs) appear only as one-line relationships to the five ("Frogs: cautious, pessimistic, logical; clashes with birdfolk idealism"; "Squirrels: careless with valuables but resourceful, friends with ratfolk through a shared value of recycling"). I could not find a published Part 3 with the other five, so the full ten-species guide, if it exists, is probably the internal world guide artists received (see section 4).

What one full entry contains, from the fetched text:

- Values, stated as a principle the folk would say themselves. Raccoons: "there is no obligation more important than self-discovery". Rabbits explain most behaviour with "It's the way it should be." Lizards: "Lizardfolk art is meant to decay, fall apart, or be burned."
- Home, as an architecture with a reason. Rabbits dig burrows with windmills on top that double as silos and mills. Raccoons hollow out tree bases with round doors, homes that work as storage for trinkets. Birds perch high or on stilts with doors open to anyone in danger. Ratfolk live in rows of capsule-like sleeping rooms around art-decorated plazas.
- Craft and magic, tied to the same values: rabbit growth magic makes plants oversized; raccoons turn junk into projectiles and barriers; rats use hypnosis and telekinesis through snail shells; birds do rescue and healing.
- Tone of speech and temperament, with a best and a worst case. Birds are "grandiose and long-winded", at worst "self-important, stubborn, and patronizing". Ratfolk "value terseness over verbosity", which others read as standoffish. Lizards are gruff and blunt and hold grudges until reconciliation is tried.
- One or two coined social terms rather than a naming scheme: birds who finish the Plumecreed are "Windcrested", ratfolk knowledge is the "World Whorl", rabbit clans are "Fluffles".
- Gear and dress: lances and a dyed feather for birds, vine knapsacks that double as bludgeons for raccoons, antler helmets for the rabbit Pawpatch Party.

What entries mostly lack: physical description (the fetch found "looks" absent for rats, lizards, raccoons, rabbits) and any per-species naming convention. So a culture entry is about 700 words on how they live, what they value, how they talk and what they make, and not about what they look like. Inference: appearance is left to real-animal appearance, which is cheap for them and is the same shortcut we would have.

[Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/planeswalkers-guide-to-bloomburrow-part-1) supplies the shared layer all species sit under: a history (the Season of Chaos, when "the big devoured the small, the powerful preyed upon the weak" and animalfolk survived by banding together underground; the Order of the Holly Leaf pact; the War of Cinders; Lily of Valley), a map of named places each tied to a species or Calamity, and a shared language note: nearly all speak a common tongue, each species has an ancestral language used only in old writing ([search summary of Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/planeswalkers-guide-to-bloomburrow-part-1)). Inference: the shared history is what lets ten unrelated species be one society; the species pages only need to say how each differs.

Communities mix: single-species hamlets, villages of species with similar lifestyles, and big cities with everyone ([Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/planeswalkers-guide-to-bloomburrow-part-2)). The species identity is therefore a tendency, not a wall.

## 2. Mechanics from culture, or culture from mechanics

Order, in Rosewater's words: mechanics-structure first, flavour layered on. The core structure "was making the ten two-color pairs interconnect in a way that played well mechanically", which he calls "a very bottom-up structure", and then "We did do a lot to flavor things accordingly" ([Handoff Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-1)). But the animal choice itself came from genre flavour: Aaron Forsythe wanted an animal world, and two takes were weighed (animals as groups, favouring factions and typal; animals as individuals, favouring card-level design); market research split evenly and "In a tie, Aaron's original vision won out" ([The Bloom of the Burrow, Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/the-bloom-of-the-burrow-part-1)). So: the animal-world premise was a top-down decision, the species were assigned to colour pairs by colour history ("We tried where possible to fit the animals in colors that they had a history in"), and the mechanics were then tuned to each animal's reputation.

The mechanical identity of each, each tied to a one-line character in the vision handoff ([Handoff Part 3](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-3)):

- Birds, white-blue: "helpful, chipper companions"; flying plus buffs and protection.
- Rats, blue-black: "cunning rogues and strategists"; saboteur triggers, evasion.
- Lizards, black-red: "twitchy, cold-blooded freaks who will bite you as soon as they look at you"; rewards for opponent life loss, pinging.
- Raccoons, red-green: "resourceful, dumpster-diving rascals"; ramp, multiple spells per turn (later "expend").
- Rabbits, green-white: "huge families, reproduce quickly, and eat a lot of vegetables"; go-wide tokens.
- Bats, white-black: "winged hunters that feed on blood"; flying, lifelink, life-change triggers.
- Otters, blue-red: "clever, playful tricksters"; noncreature spells, prowess.
- Squirrels, black-green: "scurrying hoarders who bury acorns and dig them back up later"; graveyard recursion and Food sacrifice (burying and retrieving).
- Mice, red-white: "small, brave heroes who defend their community"; go-wide targeting ("valiant").
- Frogs, green-blue: "hop, evolve over their lifetimes, and catch bugs"; bounce and flicker, with +1/+1 counters for tadpole growth, temporary flying for hopping, reach for catching insects.

How derivation worked, mechanism: each line is a stereotype a stranger already holds, converted to a verb the game has. Inference: that is the whole trick; the culture is chosen so the mechanic can be read from a one-sentence stereotype. The frogs entry shows the reverse pressure: the mechanical input (creature enters) "is a little abstract for the flavor of frogs", so the designers added outputs that carried the animal.

Where culture and mechanic fought:

- Lizards in black. Lizards had 140+ cards, mostly in red-green, but Raccoons had the better claim there, so the team kept black-red Lizards and flavoured "lizards a little darker in the world to justify Lizard cards in black" ([Bloom of the Burrow, Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/the-bloom-of-the-burrow-part-1)). Here mechanics won and the world was bent to fit.
- Birds. Flying everywhere raised a worry inherited from Lorwyn's Faeries, so Birds were kept below normal frequency and mixed with nonflyers ([Handoff Part 3](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-3)). Rosewater later admits the resulting "nonflying matters" theme "contradicted the desire for an all-Birds deck" ([State of Design 2025](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/state-of-design-2025)). Mechanics won and the animal paid.
- Squirrels. A sacrifice theme "worked against the friendlier feel of the set and was dropped" ([Bloom of the Burrow, Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/the-bloom-of-the-burrow-part-2)). Here tone won. Gift's cost was a Food token or a card, not life to the opponent, because "the flavor felt a little off", and Food stayed because it "felt festive and matched the tone" (same article).
- Spillover: Raccoons' graveyard interaction and Rabbits' Food theme were pushed to Squirrels ([Handoff Part 3](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-3)), which means an animal's flavour identity was moved for balance reasons.
- Predators. The vision file wanted dramatic predator fights; Vision Summit feedback said predators were closing out games; set design reduced them and "decided that this feeling wasn't important".

## 3. Names

Legend names. The [Legends of Bloomburrow](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/the-legends-of-bloomburrow) page lists 34 entries, 100 to 200 words each, as one paragraph of prose each. Pattern per fetch: personal name plus epithet ("Helga, Skittish Seer", "Finneas, Ace Archer", "Clement, the Worrywort", "Hazel of the Rootbloom"). Names are ordinary human-sounding or whimsical given names (Mabel, Helga, Finneas, Zoraline, Glarb, Byrke, Hugs), not species-keyed. Species is stated inside the story for only some (Helga frog, Finneas rabbit, Zoraline bat, Hugs badger), and for others (Mabel, Glarb, Bumbleflower) it is not stated in the text fetched. Inference: the species is carried by the art and the type line, so the name does not need to; there is no per-species naming convention in anything I could find. I found no source saying who named the characters or from what pool; the story is credited to Valerie Valdes ([Magic Story](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/magic-story/episode-1-calamity-comes-to-valley)) and the Wizards set page credits Clayton Kroh for the set's announcement copy ([First Look](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/announcements/a-first-look-at-bloomburrow)), which is not the same as naming credit.

A naming mechanism that is stated: names come out of in-world process. Hugs's name "was given by children after an act of kindness" (Legends page, as fetched), and Bello's moniker "omentrotter" is a bard's nickname. Fame comes from deeds, songs, rumour. Inference: names get a story reason inside the world, so a name can be explained in one line.

Mice are the human stand-in: "the stand-in for humans (i.e., the base creature type in Magic) would be mice" ([Handoff Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-1)). Their legend, Mabel, is the protagonist.

Calamity Beasts, the animals with no society. Origin: "the Creative team came up with the concept of Calamity Beasts, which were elemental forces responsible for seasonal change, essentially living weather events" ([Handoff Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-1)). They are "towering, wordless creatures", non-sapient, and "Bloomburrow has no natural seasons"; seasons are named after the beast that causes them (Blizzard Elk, Sun Hawk) ([Guide Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/planeswalkers-guide-to-bloomburrow-part-1)). Major ones are unique and named, minor ones are not. On the Legends page each Calamity Beast gets one in-world quotation of 70 to 120 words from a bard or witness, not a narrated biography. Mechanically they carry the Elemental type, not an animal type. Lesson for naming: an animal that has no society is named by what it does to the society (a season, a weather), through the mouths of the animalfolk who fear it, and its name is a plain "animal plus force" (Blizzard Elk). Inference: the plainness of the animal word is what keeps it a real animal, and the power word is what makes it a legend.

Names that changed or failed: I found no statement about legend names that were changed. Mechanic names have a trail: classes became "talents" "because not all of the cards represent trades"; the opponent-benefit mechanic became "gift" for an in-world reason ([Bloom of the Burrow, Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/the-bloom-of-the-burrow-part-2)). A puns-heavy tone in card names was a draw for some players ([Cool Stuff Inc.](https://www.coolstuffinc.com/a/jayannelli-08012024-the-flavor-gems-of-bloomburrow)).

## 4. Art: a civilisation with no humans

Direction, as the art director described it ([Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/magic-the-gathering/mtg-art-director-bloomburrow-cute)): Zack Stella wanted animals that look like animals, not cartoons, "so much was important about these looking like animals and not humans". Briefs asked artists to specify fur colour, pattern or scales, and the aim was a zoo-visit variety of species. Cuteness was policed: "The animals are cute enough on their own, so we wanted to see them doing combat things over seeing them napping or being intentionally cute on top of that." Another voice on the team calls Stella "pretty militant about keeping an eye on the little details". Tone stays Magic: "When you see squirrelfolk, even if it's a cute squirrel, it's pretty awesome, pretty terrifying." Cuteness was deliberately concentrated in one place, the baby tokens of the offspring mechanic.

Scale: animalfolk are the size of their real species (so a boulder is a mountain and trees hold cities, [Guide Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/planeswalkers-guide-to-bloomburrow-part-1)), and card power follows it: mice 0/1 to 2/2, raccoons up to about 4/4, every predator at least 4 power or toughness, no base stat 7 or more ([Handoff Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-1)). The art style was loosened from Magic's "imaginative realism" toward gestural styles ([WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/hereandnow/2024/07/26/bloomburrow-magic-the-gathering)). Costuming, weapons and gear were governed by a world guide that artists called clear: Mouse Guard's David Petersen said the material "was all presented wonderfully for easy reference", it covered "the characters and their costuming, weapons", and the art director gave "clear and descriptive" direction per illustration ([MMORPG interview](https://www.mmorpg.com/interviews/the-art-of-magic-the-gatherings-bloomburrow-two-guests-artists-speak-on-drawing-mtg-2000132394)). Petersen also said the ten base species being dual-coloured "help codify their strengths and personas", and that for Mabel he had to make his stylised mouse "look more like a real-world mouse". WBUR notes his mice needed "the correct amount of fingers and toes" and more accurate ears.

The human-made question. This is not a world with nothing made: the folk wear clothes and armour, hold lances, ladles, ladders, ceramic and glass ember containers, and live in windmills and inns. The rule is not "no tools", it is "no human things", and every object belongs to a species culture (windmill silos for rabbits, snail-shell conduits for rats, ember containers for lizards). Inference: Bloomburrow's rule is therefore the opposite of ours on tools, and the nearest thing to ours is the real-animal-first rule on bodies.

What I could not find: a published list of forbidden things, a settlement-per-species visual guide, or how Calamity Beasts were painted. The Arita brief for Lumra, a bear, is the best sample of how a non-society animal is briefed: "A massive BEAR (see attached) is roaring. Its roar is so powerful it causes the trees around it to bend." That is one real animal, one verb, one effect on the world.

Criticisms of art: I found none about "too human"; the criticism is the reverse tension, art softer than play. WBUR writes that it "never felt so hot on a Magic product's art and so lukewarm on its mix of mechanics".

## 5. Legendaries as characters

How much story a legend gets: the public Legends page gives a 100 to 200 word paragraph (Calamity Beasts one quote), with art asset codes, no stats ([Legends of Bloomburrow](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/the-legends-of-bloomburrow)). The paragraph is built from background, a relationship, a deed and the role in the quest (Finneas opens: "Known as a crack shot throughout the village of Goodhill but not much farther"). Inference: that is a very thin character, one sentence of reputation plus one of motive, and it is what an artist or name-writer needs to read in under a minute.

Who reads it: the Creative team is cited as feeding design. Neale LaPlante Johnson, creative and story designer, "did a great job making us aware of cool creative concepts and story characters that inspired great top-down card designs" ([Bloom of the Burrow, Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/the-bloom-of-the-burrow-part-2)). Ten rare legends carry the ten species' creature types, one per pair ([MTG Wiki, via search result, not verified on the page](https://mtg.fandom.com/wiki/Bloomburrow)), and the set then adds story characters on top. I found no source describing, step by step, how a one-line character became abilities; Rosewater's general statement is "top-down card designs" from story characters, and the species-level mapping in section 2 does the rest (the legend inherits the colour pair, so most of the ability space is already fixed by the species).

Inference on why this works: because the legend's colour pair is fixed by species, the designer is never asked "what does this character do" from nothing, only "which of the species verbs does this individual push to an extreme".

## 6. Reception and failure

Positive: Rosewater called cross-team coordination "a homerun" and wrote that "The Rabbits felt like Rabbits and the Frogs felt like Frogs" ([State of Design 2025](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/state-of-design-2025)). Players praised the Calamity Beasts as "such a cool and inspired touch for this setting" and liked Frogs and Squirrels ([EDHREC, one year later](https://edhrec.com/articles/examining-bloomburrow-one-year-later)). Wikipedia lists the reception as very positive ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomburrow)).

Failure, from Rosewater himself (same State of Design article): draft was "too 'on rails'"; "Too many of the animals we focused on don't have enough cards" (Commander); Otters "weren't powerful enough"; Mice "were too powerful"; Rats "felt off to some". Most telling for us is the structural criticism he reports: players said "We should have just had five animal factions", or that "Limiting each archetype to one animal was too limiting", and his own conclusion that factions "should have been about something that could group together numerous animals." That is a verdict on the unit of society: one species per society was too narrow, and the animals were chosen partly for pair fit rather than for being the best society.

Not found: any player complaint that the societies were not felt through cards alone, or "too cute" complaints. A third-party reviewer calls the set "fuzzy" and says "a lot was missing" ([EDHREC](https://edhrec.com/articles/examining-bloomburrow-one-year-later)); that is about gameplay.

## Contrast: Lorwyn and Ikoria

Lorwyn built tribes (kithkin, elves, boggarts and others) around folklore sources, and Rosewater says changelings were "the key ingredient that makes Lorwyn's tribal design work", and that colours for each tribe were stretched, including into colours the race had barely been in ([Lorwyn summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorwyn)). In the Bloomburrow handoff, R&D's view had shifted: "Innistrad was the better way to do typal themes as opposed to how Lorwyn did them" ([Handoff Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/bloomburrow-vision-design-handoff-part-2)). Bloomburrow responded by having only two changelings and ten "duo" cards that name two animals as friends. The lesson, inference: species-as-faction needs a glue device.

Ikoria set monsters as the subject, inspired by kaiju, "bonding with monsters" ([Ikoria handoff summary](https://articles.starcitygames.com/?p=45107)). I did not find enough on how Ikoria's monsters were named or given societies to say more; it is a contrast of monsters-as-partners rather than monsters-as-peoples, and the Calamity Beasts sit closer to it than to the animalfolk.

## What transfers to Animal Kingdom and what does not

- Transfers: the shared history layer. Ten species were made one society by one story (Season of Chaos, banding underground), with species pages stating only the difference. Our societies need one "what has happened" that all share, then pages that are deltas. Reason: it is cheaper and stops the pages from contradicting each other.
- Transfers: culture entries are about values, home, craft, tone and coined terms, not looks. Their ~700 words per species suggests one page per society is the right size. Reason: appearance comes free from the real animal, as it does for us.
- Transfers: each entry states values as something the folk would say ("It's the way it should be"), plus a best and worst case of temperament. Reason: a name writer or effect designer can test a candidate against a sentence in the species' own voice.
- Transfers: the stereotype-to-verb mapping (squirrel buries and retrieves, rabbit multiplies). Reason: it let a single line fix colour, mechanic and legend. Our animals have the same readable public reputations (honey badger, rat, queen bee).
- Transfers with care: Calamity Beasts show how to treat animals with no society: named for what they do to the world (Blizzard Elk), spoken of by witnesses, one quote each. Reason: the name is plain and the force carries the legend. Our solitary legends could take the same route.
- Transfers: art briefs that specify real-animal facts (fur, pattern, scale) and one verb, and keep cuteness for one place. Reason: it is the working form of "animals not humans" and held up with a famous mouse artist.
- Does not transfer: animalfolk clothes, armour, windmills, inns, ladles. Reason: our animals are real animals and the world contains nothing human-made; their rule banned human things, not tools, and we have to find our own answer for what a society makes (nests, tunnels, hoards, scent marks), which they did not need to solve.
- Does not transfer: one species equals one society tied to a colour pair. Reason: it came from the ten-colour-pair structure; Wizards' own hindsight says it was too narrow. Our decks follow families and habitats, so the unit is already closer to what Rosewater says he wanted.
- Does not transfer: the order "mechanics structure first, flavour layered on". Reason: it was a limited-format necessity for a large set; our hypothesis is the reverse, and Bloomburrow's lizards-in-black case shows the cost of bending the world to the cards.
- Does not transfer: the thin legend paragraph as sufficient. Reason: their legends had species, colour pair and a shared set of species verbs already fixed; ours are named individuals with a written character and carry more of the weight alone.

## Open questions the sources did not answer

- Who named the legends, from what pool, and whether any names were rejected. Nothing found.
- The full ten-species world guide given to artists was not found; only five culture entries are public. How long and what fields the internal one has is unknown.
- How a legend's one-paragraph character became its abilities, step by step. Only the general "top-down" claim was found.
- Whether any player or critic felt the societies through cards alone without the story; no source tested it.
- Any art-side list of forbidden things, per-species settlement looks, or how Calamity Beasts were briefed beyond Lumra.
- Ikoria's species-level treatment beyond its kaiju theme, and Lorwyn's tribe culture write-ups, were not read in primary form.
- The tool that fetched pages summarised them; the exact quotes should be re-checked before they go into a decision document.
