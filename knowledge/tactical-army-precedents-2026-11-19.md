# Tactical boards with collected armies: who tried it and how it went (research of 2026-11-19)

A Sonnet web-research pass, asked when Martin weighed today's game (a collectible card game deployed onto a hex board, with covering) against "deckbuilding chess" (open armies starting on the board, no draw, one move a turn). Few postmortems exist; most numbers are third-party tracker estimates and say so.

## Digital card games with a tactical board (today's game)

- Duelyst (2016-2020): 5x9 grid, praised as one of the best post-Hearthstone card games; peak about 2,400 concurrent players (SteamDB), servers closed February 2020 on declining players ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duelyst)). The fan relaunch Duelyst II (2022) with free code and art peaked near 1,900 and sits in single or low double digits ([SteamCharts](https://steamcharts.com/app/2004320)).
- Faeria (2016-): hex board, the closest in spirit; peak about 3,700 (SteamDB), now tens. Its COO: the $20 early-access months were probably the best revenue, "things only got worse when we shifted to free-to-play"; back to buy-to-play in 2018 ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/f2p-pay-play-faeria-successfully-212400243.html)).
- Krosmaga (Ankama, 2016-): development frozen in 2019. Mythgard: development halted 2021. Artifact (Valve, 2018): 60,000 concurrent at launch, about 1,500 two months later, stopped 2021, blamed on paid packs and a heavy first game ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_(video_game))).
- Covering: the only verified stacking is Hive's beetle; no digital card game found that plays cards on top of enemy units ("not found", not "does not exist").

## No luck, open information

- Prismata (2014-): no decks, no hidden information; the team rebalanced over a dozen times in about three years; its designer says luck helps retention and monetisation; very positive reviews, single to low double digit concurrent players now ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prismata)).
- Chess 2: The Sequel (2013-14): six asymmetric armies; mixed reviews; an estimate of about 3,200 copies on Steam (rough).
- Hive (2001): no luck, 20 minutes, world championship since 2007; a small, durable niche.

## Collected armies on a board (the figurine side)

- HeroScape (2004-2010): prepainted figures on hex terrain, point-built armies; discontinued in 2010; a $250 relaunch campaign failed in 2022 (4,300 of 8,000 backers); relaunched by Renegade in 2024, Wave 1 sold out ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heroscape), [ComicBook](https://comicbook.com/gaming/news/heroscape-age-of-annihilation-dead-shelved-indefinitely)).
- Mage Knight (2000) and HeroClix (2002): collectible miniatures; Mage Knight declined on staleness, balance, limited editions and owner changes; HeroClix is the longest survivor ([MeepleShelter](https://meepleshelter.net/mage-knight-the-rise-and-fall-of-one-of-the-most-successful-miniature-games-ever/)).

## Chess with different or collected armies

- Gliński's hexagonal chess (1936): reportedly 500,000 players, one fixed army. Betza's Chess with Different Armies: a known variant, niche like nearly all variants.
- Roguelike chess sells (Shotgun King, an estimated 145,000 to 307,000 copies, 91% positive), all single-player. No released competitive collected-army chess found for 2020-2026; Pieces of the Kingdom (PvP) is listed for September 2026.
- Auto Chess (2019): 8 million players in five months; chess-like boards reach millions when they are cheap to learn.

## The pattern

Failures: small audiences, copying Hearthstone's free-to-play model, small teams and long balancing, too few players online for matchmaking, a heavy first game. Survivors: a premium price with every card obtainable (Faeria), devoted small scenes and tournaments, and play that doesn't need a full queue (bots, asynchronous, local, single-player).
